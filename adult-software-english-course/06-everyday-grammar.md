# Common Grammar: Understand, Compare, and Use

## Route and practice method

This supplements the existing lessons; it does not replace their basic tense, question, and pronunciation teaching. Common means useful for everyday life and ordinary software collaboration, not an exact frequency ranking. Start with the [foundation workbook](08-foundation-workbook.md) alongside the lessons, then select material here by the priorities below. Revisit earlier sections whenever a task exposes a gap. Do not finish this page before starting to speak.

| Priority | Material on this page | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Revisit during foundations | Sections 1-4: sentence jobs, articles and quantity, pronouns and agreement, comparison and prepositions; direct questions and negatives in Section 5 | Alongside Units 1-12; one small distinction at a time, not a whole section per sitting |
| Practise after foundations stabilize | Embedded questions in Section 5; common passives in Section 7; verb combinations in Section 8; content, relative, time, reason and condition clauses in Section 9; simple reported messages and short answers in Section 12 | Alongside Units 13-20; one communicative use per session, short sentences before combinations |
| Learn common meanings first | Past versus present perfect and future expressions in Section 6; real conditions and hypothetical options in Section 10; requests, suggestions, obligations and current possibilities in Section 11 | Use corresponding foundation stations, then select these paragraphs first |
| Add later as needed | Perfect continuous, past perfect and future continuous in Section 6; past counterfactuals and regrets in Section 10; past deductions in Section 11; tag questions in Section 12 | Separate sessions when work or reading repeatedly requires them; not prerequisites for beginner speech |

For example, do not cover all three clause types in Section 9 in one day. Start with I think ..., then the file that ..., then contrast because / if / when. In Section 8, establish need to check, enjoy reading, and thanks for checking before comparing remember/stop/try. Recognizing the later-priority material is enough to continue the main lessons initially; you need not answer all four section exercises before proceeding.

Spend one short session on the explanation and four exercises, and another on the transfer task. For every model: understand it, hear it through a partner or TTS, cover it, and change one fact. There is no bundled audio. Write answers before opening the key. A grammatically correct answer with the wrong time, negative, or factual claim still needs repair. The four questions in each section have a key; the open speaking/writing task has observable checks rather than one compulsory script.

## 1. Word classes and sentence jobs

A word class tells what kind of word something is; a sentence job tells what it does in this sentence. `test` can be a noun in `The test passed` or a verb in `We test it`. Do not classify a word from its spelling alone.

| Class | Job in these examples | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Noun / pronoun | Name something / stand for it | a server; it |
| Verb | Express an action or state | run; be; need |
| Adjective | Describe a noun or a subject after a linking verb | a slow server; the server is slow |
| Adverb | Modify an action, adjective, or statement | run slowly; very slow; usually |
| Determiner | Mark which thing or how much | a; the; my; this; some |
| Preposition | Introduce a relationship | in the room; after lunch |
| Conjunction | Connect words or clauses | and; because; although |

In `Our new app sends users a message every morning`, `Our new app` is the subject; `sends` is the main verb; `users` is the indirect object; `a message` is the direct object; `every morning` gives time. `new` modifies app. The subject is not always just the first word.

Five useful patterns: `The app runs` (subject + verb); `The app is ready` (subject + linking verb + subject complement); `We check the result` (subject + verb + object); `We send users a message` (two objects); `The change makes the page faster` (object + object complement). A complement completes a meaning such as what something is or becomes; an adverbial adds information such as when, where, or how.

You can say `Send me the file` or `Send the file to me`. But say `Explain the problem to me`, not `Explain me the problem`: the two-object pattern is not available with every verb. `It is cold` uses a grammatical subject even when there is no object called “it.”

Practice: 1. Identify the subject and main verb in `The two new tests passed yesterday`. 2. Repair `The page very slow`. 3. Repair `Please explain me the error`. 4. In `The fix makes the test reliable`, what does `reliable` describe?

<details>
<summary>Section 1 answers</summary>

1. Subject: The two new tests; verb: passed. 2. The page is very slow. English needs the linking verb here. 3. Please explain the error to me. 4. The test, not the fix; it is the object complement.

</details>

Transfer: write one sentence for each of the five patterns, then say three without reading. A partner should identify who acts and what is affected. Use the same simple vocabulary so you practise structure rather than translation.

## 2. Articles, quantity, and choosing a noun phrase

For a singular countable noun in an ordinary sentence, choose a determiner: `a request`, `the request`, `this request`, `my request`. Do not combine them as `the my request`. Use a/an when introducing a non-specific example, the when the listener can identify it, and no article for general plural or uncountable nouns: `Tests take time. Software needs maintenance.`

Compare `I need a test account` (one suitable account) with `I need the test account Sara created` (identified account). `a university` and `an hour` depend on the next sound, not the letter. A name often needs no article (`Mina`, `Monday`), but names and fixed expressions have exceptions: learn `at home`, `go to work`, `in the office` as phrases.

`information`, `advice`, `equipment`, and software in its ordinary mass sense are uncountable: `some advice`, `two pieces of equipment`. `work` is uncountable for general work, but `a task` is countable. A noun can change meaning: `coffee` as a substance, `two coffees` as two servings.

| Meaning | Countable plural | Uncountable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large quantity | many requests | much traffic |
| Some, a useful amount | a few requests | a little time |
| Very little, often insufficient | few requests | little time |
| Smaller quantity | fewer requests | less traffic |

`a lot of` works for both. `some` often introduces an amount; `any` is common in questions and negatives, but `Would you like some tea?` is a normal offer. `each/every file` takes a singular noun and verb. `both files` means two; `either file` means one or the other; `neither file` means not one and not the other. `another test` is one more/different test; `the other test` is the remaining identified one; `other tests` is plural. `All tests did not pass` can be unclear: say `No tests passed` or `Some tests failed` according to the facts.

Practice: 1. Fill articles: `I found ___ error. ___ error is in the title`. 2. Repair `We need two advices`. 3. Choose: `We have fewer / less time today`. 4. Repair `Every files are ready`.

<details>
<summary>Section 2 answers</summary>

1. an; the. 2. We need two pieces of advice. / We need some advice if the number is not important. 3. less. 4. Every file is ready. Each file is ready also works, with individual emphasis.

</details>

Transfer: request one new account, refer to that same account again, and compare two workloads using fewer and less. Check that the listener knows which account you mean.

## 3. Pronouns and subject-verb agreement

Review I/me/my/mine in the quick reference. `myself`, `yourself`, `himself`, `herself`, `itself`, `ourselves`, `yourselves`, and `themselves` are reflexives: `She introduced herself`. They can add emphasis: `I checked it myself`. Do not use myself merely to sound formal: `Send it to Lee and me`, not `to Lee and myself` in that ordinary request. `We help each other` describes mutual help, not each person helping themselves.

`it` refers to the same thing: `I opened the file and read it`. `one` can replace a countable noun: `This file is old. I need a new one`. `ones` is plural. Use a noun again when a pronoun is ambiguous: `Mina told Jo that her test failed` does not make ownership clear.

Find the head of the subject, not the nearest noun: `The list of errors is long`; `The errors in the list are serious`. Ordinary `and` subjects are plural: `Mina and Jo are ready`. `Everyone is ready` and `Each test has a name` take singular verbs. `There are two errors`; `There is one error`. With a person using singular they, use `they are/have`, not `they is/has`. Collective nouns vary by dialect and focus; use `The team members are ...` when you want an unambiguous plural.

Practice: 1. Choose: `The results of this test is / are ready`. 2. Repair `Everyone have an account`. 3. Fill: `I need a newer laptop. This ___ is too slow`. 4. Make ownership explicit: `Rae told Kim that her account was locked` when Kim owns the locked account.

<details>
<summary>Section 3 answers</summary>

1. are; the head is results. 2. Everyone has an account. 3. one. 4. Rae told Kim that Kim's account was locked. Repeating the name is better than leaving the owner uncertain.

</details>

Transfer: describe two objects using it/one, then write an update naming an owner without an ambiguous pronoun. The reader must identify the same object and owner you intended.

## 4. Adjectives, adverbs, comparison, and prepositions

Use an adjective for a thing or its state: `a careful reviewer`, `The explanation sounds clear`. Use an adverb for how an action happens: `She checks carefully`, `He explains clearly`. Linking verbs such as seem, look, and sound commonly take adjectives. Not all adverbs end in -ly: `The app runs fast`; friendly is normally an adjective. `hard` means with effort or intensity; `hardly` means almost not: `I can hardly hear you`.

Put degree words before adjectives: `very useful`, `too slow`, `clear enough`. `enough` goes before nouns: `enough time`. `too slow` normally means slower than is acceptable for the purpose, not simply very slow. Use `too slow to use` and `fast enough to use` to connect a judgment to a task.

Compare `faster`, `more reliable`, `better`, `worse`; not `more faster`. `as reliable as` means equal on that criterion; `not as reliable as` means less reliable. `much faster` and `a little faster` express degree; not `very faster`. `the fastest option` needs a relevant comparison set, not a claim about every system in the world.

Learn prepositions as combinations: `depend on access`, `wait for a reply`, `reply to a message`, `good at testing`, `interested in learning`. Say `discuss the problem`, not `discuss about the problem`. After a preposition use a noun or -ing: `before deploying`, `without changing it`. `to` can be a preposition in `look forward to meeting you`, not an infinitive marker.

Practice: 1. Choose: `The explanation sounds clearly / clear`. 2. Repair `This build runs more faster`. 3. Repair `We don't have time enough`. 4. Repair `I'm interested in learn more`.

<details>
<summary>Section 4 answers</summary>

1. clear. 2. This build runs faster. 3. We don't have enough time. 4. I'm interested in learning more.

</details>

Transfer: compare two fictional tools on speed and reliability, then explain one limitation with too or enough. State the comparison as a practice scenario, not as an unsupported real benchmark.

## 5. Questions, negatives, and useful sentence frames

The quick reference distinguishes be, do/does/did, and modal questions. Compare `Who changed it?` (who is the subject, no ordinary do needed) and `What did you change?` (you is the subject). `How long does it take?` asks duration; `How often does it fail?` asks frequency; `How many attempts failed?` asks a count.

An embedded question uses statement order: `Do you know why it failed?`, `Could you tell me where the file is?` For yes/no content use if/whether: `Please check whether the account is active`. Do not interpret this whether as a condition.

Negatives have scope: `I don't think it is ready` usually conveys a negative view about readiness. `Not all tests passed` means at least some failed, not necessarily that every test failed. `We haven't approved it yet` does not mean approval will certainly come later. Avoid double negatives in standard workplace messages: `I didn't see anything`, not `I didn't see nothing` for that meaning.

Use `It takes ten minutes to run the test`, `It is useful to record the steps`, `There is a problem`, and `I have a problem`. These are different patterns, not interchangeable translations. For negative instructions use `Don't close the page`. Polite request: `Could you keep it open?`

Practice: 1. Repair `Who did changed the setting?` for a neutral subject question. 2. Repair `Do you know where is the log?` 3. Repair `There have two errors`. 4. You know only three of five tests passed. Repair `No tests passed` without overstating the evidence.

<details>
<summary>Section 5 answers</summary>

1. Who changed the setting? 2. Do you know where the log is? 3. There are two errors. 4. Three of five tests passed; the other two did not pass. / Not all tests passed. The precise count is usually more useful.

</details>

Transfer: ask about duration, frequency, and a missing location. Have the partner answer a different question once; clarify which information you need rather than repeating louder.

## 6. Time: completion, duration, and earlier events

First review present simple, continuous, past simple, and future choices in the quick reference. Then add the contrasts below. A tense is selected from the intended timeline, not a single “signal word.”

| Contrast | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Past event / current result | I fixed it yesterday. / I've fixed it. | Finished past time / current completion claim |
| Ongoing or recent activity | I've been investigating it for an hour. | Focus on activity continuing up to now or recently stopping |
| Result or count | I've checked three logs. | Three checks completed; not merely time spent |
| Earlier than a past point | The service had stopped before I opened the page. | Past perfect: had + past participle |
| At a future point | I'll be testing at two. | will be + -ing; activity expected in progress then |

Present perfect continuous: `have/has been + -ing`; negative `I haven't been working on that`; question `How long have you been waiting?` It does not guarantee an activity is still happening: `I've been running, so I'm tired` can describe recent activity that just stopped. State verbs commonly use perfect simple: `I've known her for years`, not normally `I've been knowing her`. `I've worked here for two years` and `I've been working here for two years` can both describe ongoing employment with different emphasis.

Past perfect: `When we started the demo, the session had expired`. Negative: `It hadn't expired`; question: `Had it expired before the demo?` Do not change every earlier sentence to past perfect. A clear sequence can use past simple: `We opened the page, signed in, and ran the test`.

`used to + base verb` describes a past habit/state no longer true: `I used to work at night`; `Did you use to work at night?`; `I didn't use to work at night`. `be used to + noun/-ing` means be accustomed to: `I'm used to working remotely`. `get used to` means become accustomed to.

For futures, `will` can be a decision or offer, `going to` an intention, present continuous an arrangement, and present simple a timetable: `The train leaves at six`. Context matters more than claiming a fixed certainty ranking.

Practice: 1. Repair `I've been knowing this tool for years`. 2. Use past perfect: the session expired first; then the demo began. Start `When the demo began, ...` 3. Repair `I'm used to work remotely`. 4. You have completed exactly two checks. Choose `I've checked two logs` or `I've been checking logs` to report the count.

<details>
<summary>Section 6 answers</summary>

1. I've known this tool for years. More natural in many contexts: I've used this tool for years. 2. When the demo began, the session had expired. 3. I'm used to working remotely. 4. I've checked two logs. The continuous sentence does not state the count.

</details>

Transfer: give a four-sentence update with a finished event, a duration, an earlier past event, and a future arrangement. A partner should draw the correct order and identify anything still unfinished.

## 7. Passive voice and responsibility

Active: `Jo approved the change`. Passive: `The change was approved by Jo`. The object becomes the subject; be carries tense/agreement, and the action verb is a past participle. Use a passive when the affected thing matters or the actor is unknown. Name the actor when ownership is important.

| Time or function | Passive example |
| --- | --- |
| Routine | The logs are checked daily. |
| Past | The account was created yesterday. |
| In progress | The issue is being investigated. |
| Completed | The change has been approved. |
| Future | The result will be shared tomorrow. |
| Requirement | The input must be checked. |

Negative and question: `It hasn't been approved`; `Has it been approved?` `The test passed` is an active result statement. `The test was passed by all candidates` describes people succeeding in a test. Do not mechanically add was to every past verb. Some verbs have no object in the relevant meaning: say `The error occurred`, not `was occurred`.

`The door is closed` may describe a state; `The door is being closed` describes an action in progress. `The issue is fixed` and `The fix has been verified` are not interchangeable evidence claims. Grammar must preserve the actual status.

Practice: 1. Make passive: `Rin created the account yesterday`. 2. Repair `The logs are checking now` when someone is checking them. 3. Repair `The error was occurred at noon`. 4. Ask whether approval has happened, using a passive question.

<details>
<summary>Section 7 answers</summary>

1. The account was created by Rin yesterday. 2. The logs are being checked now. 3. The error occurred at noon. 4. Has it been approved? / Has the change been approved?

</details>

Transfer: write one action owner sentence and one passive status sentence about the same task. Check that required, in progress, and approved are not confused.

## 8. Infinitives, -ing forms, and participles

A second verb does not always use the same form. Learn the controlling word and phrase, then reuse it: `want/plan/decide/hope to check`; `enjoy/avoid/finish/suggest checking`; `ask someone to check`; `let someone check`; `make someone wait`. `help someone check` and `help someone to check` are both possible.

An infinitive can express purpose: `I opened the log to find the error`. An -ing phrase can be a subject: `Testing takes time`. After a preposition: `Thanks for checking`; `before sending`; `without restarting`. Do not confuse a noun-like -ing phrase with a progressive verb: `Checking takes time` versus `I am checking`.

| Contrast | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| stop testing / stop to test | End testing / stop another activity in order to test |
| remember to save / remember saving | Remember the required future action / recall a past action |
| try restarting / try to restart | Experiment with restarting as a solution / attempt the restart |
| interested person / interesting task | Person feels interest / task causes interest |

Participles can describe nouns: `a broken link`, `a missing file`, `the person reviewing the change`. For an introductory action phrase, keep the actor clear: `After checking the log, I found the error`. `After checking the log, the error was obvious` misleadingly attaches checking to the error; use `After I checked the log, the error was obvious`.

Practice: 1. Repair `I suggest to add a test`. 2. Repair `Thanks for help me`. 3. Choose for a future reminder: `Remember to save / saving the file before leaving`. 4. Repair `After reading the report, the problem was clear` by naming the reader as I.

<details>
<summary>Section 8 answers</summary>

1. I suggest adding a test. 2. Thanks for helping me. 3. to save. 4. After I read the report, the problem was clear. / After reading the report, I understood the problem.

</details>

Transfer: give an instruction with purpose, thank someone for an action, and suggest an experiment. The listener should distinguish trying a remedy from guaranteeing it works.

## 9. Common clauses: content, description, and circumstances

A clause usually contains a subject and a verb. A main clause can be a complete statement; a subordinate clause fits inside or adds meaning to another. First locate each subject and verb, then identify the connection.

Content clauses tell what someone thinks, says, or asks: `I think (that) the test passed`; `We need to know whether it passed`; `I don't know what changed`. In the last example, what is the subject of changed. `I know what you changed` instead has you as its subject. You can often omit that after think/say, but keep it when it improves clarity.

Relative clauses identify a person or thing: `the colleague who helped me`, `the file that failed`, `the tool which we use`. Use whose for possession: `the user whose account is locked`. An object relative can be omitted: `the tool we use`; a subject relative cannot: `the tool that runs daily`, not `the tool runs daily` within that noun phrase. Do not repeat the object: `the file that I opened`, not `that I opened it`.

Commas distinguish extra information: `My laptop, which is new, is slow` adds information about an already identified laptop. `The laptop that is on the desk is slow` identifies which one. In standard non-defining relative clauses, use who/which, not that, and do not omit the relative word. In speech, two short sentences are often easier.

Adverbial clauses express time, reason, contrast, or condition: `When it finishes, I'll call`; `Because access is missing, testing is blocked`; `Although it passed, we still need approval`; `If it fails, tell me`. After a future time when/until/as soon as, normally use a present form: `I'll reply as soon as I know`. `because + clause` versus `because of + noun`: `because the network failed` / `because of a network failure`. `unless` means “except if” in simple conditions: `We won't proceed unless access is approved` does not itself guarantee proceeding after approval.

Practice: 1. Repair `I don't know why did it fail`. 2. Repair `The file that I opened it is empty`. 3. Join with although: `The test passed. Approval is still pending`. 4. Repair `We paused because of the account was locked`.

<details>
<summary>Section 9 answers</summary>

1. I don't know why it failed. 2. The file that I opened is empty. 3. Although the test passed, approval is still pending. 4. We paused because the account was locked. / We paused because of the locked account.

</details>

Transfer: read `The report that Jo sent says that testing is blocked because access is missing`. Identify which report, its message, and the reason. Then express the same facts as three short spoken sentences.

## 10. Real conditions, hypothetical choices, and regrets

| Situation | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General rule | if + present, present | If a file is empty, this tool rejects it. |
| Open future possibility | if + present, will/can + base | If access arrives, we can start. |
| Imagined present/future | if + past form, would/could + base | If we reduced the scope, we could finish sooner. |
| Different imagined past | if + had + participle, would/could have + participle | If we had checked earlier, we might have found it sooner. |

In hypothetical conditions, the past form marks distance from reality, not necessarily past time. `If I were you, I'd ask` is a common advice pattern; were works for all persons in this hypothetical use. `If I was ...` also occurs in informal speech, but learn were for this pattern. Don't normally put would in the if-clause: `If we had more time, we would test more`, not `If we would have more time ...`.

`wish + past` expresses a different desired present: `I wish I knew the answer`. `wish + had + participle` expresses past regret: `I wish I had asked earlier`. For a realistic desired future, use hope: `I hope we finish tomorrow`. `I wish you would ...` can express dissatisfaction; do not treat it as a neutral workplace request.

Past counterfactuals are useful in retrospectives, but language alone does not establish a cause. Say `might have prevented` when uncertain, not an unsupported `would definitely have prevented`. Ask what could be improved without using grammar to assign blame.

Practice: 1. Repair `If we will get access, we can start`. 2. Complete an imagined present: `If I ___ (know) the answer, I ___ (tell) you`. 3. Express regret about not saving: `I wish I ___ (save) the file`. 4. Choose for a realistic future goal: `I hope / wish we finish tomorrow`.

<details>
<summary>Section 10 answers</summary>

1. If we get access, we can start. 2. knew; would tell. 3. had saved. 4. hope. These meanings differ; the forms are not interchangeable levels of politeness.

</details>

Transfer: discuss an actual dependency, an imagined smaller scope, and a past improvement opportunity. A partner must distinguish what is real, proposed, and impossible to change now.

## 11. Modals: requests, obligations, and evidence

Review must not versus don't have to in the quick reference. Add `should` for advice, `may/might/could` for possibility, and `would` for polite preferences: `I'd prefer a smaller change`; `Would you check this?` There is no fixed numerical probability attached to might versus may. `Can you ...?` can also be a request, not only a test of ability.

Compare `We must test it` (obligation) and `It must be a configuration issue` (strong inference). The second is not direct evidence. `It can't be the same file` can mean the speaker considers it impossible, not that the file lacks an ability. To report uncertainty accurately, use `It may be ...; we haven't confirmed it`.

Past forms use modal + have + past participle: `It might have failed earlier` (possible past event); `We should have checked` (past action was advisable, usually implying we did not check); `They must have changed it` (strong inference); `It can't have finished that quickly` (inference of impossibility). `could have` can mean a past possibility or an unused opportunity; clarify context instead of treating it as an observed event.

`could` describes general past ability: `I could read simple messages then`. For a particular achieved action, `I was able to restore the sample file` or `I managed to restore it` often makes success clearer. Do not report a successful action just from “could.”

Practice: 1. Express no obligation: `You ___ reply today; tomorrow is fine`. 2. Repair `It might has failed`. 3. Explain the difference between `We should check` and `We should have checked`. 4. Evidence is insufficient. Repair `The network definitely caused it` using might and a statement of uncertainty.

<details>
<summary>Section 11 answers</summary>

1. don't have to / don't need to. 2. It might have failed. 3. Advice about checking now/later versus a judgment about a past missed or advisable action. 4. The network might have caused it, but the cause has not been confirmed. This does not prove the network was involved.

</details>

Transfer: make a polite request, state a requirement, and report one uncertain explanation. Ask the listener which sentence is a rule and which is only a hypothesis.

## 12. Reported speech and natural short responses

Reporting requires attention to speaker, time, and source: Mina said on Monday, `I'll send it tomorrow`. On Wednesday you can report `Mina said she would send it on Tuesday`. This reports her statement, not proof that she sent it. Past reporting often shifts am/is to was, will to would, and have done to had done. A still-current fact can stay present when appropriate: `Jo said the office is closed on Sundays`.

Use `say something` and `tell someone something`: `She said she was ready`; `She told me she was ready`, not `She said me ...`. Report questions with statement order: `She asked whether I had access`; `She asked when I could start`. For requests: `She asked me to check`; `She told me not to close the page`.

Short replies are ordinary, not bad grammar: `Have you sent it? Yes, I have`; `Will you join? I think so`; `Is it ready? I don't think so`. Use do to avoid repeating an ordinary verb: `Rae uses this tool, and Kim does too`. `So do I` agrees with a positive ordinary-verb statement; `Neither do I` agrees with a negative one. Match the auxiliary: `I'm ready. So am I`. `I haven't tried it. Neither have I`.

Tag questions are common in conversation: `It's ready, isn't it?`; `You didn't send it, did you?` They can seek confirmation or convey an expectation. Intonation matters. For high-stakes confirmation, use a direct question and a full answer: `Has it been approved? It has not been approved`. Do not rely on an ambiguous yes after a negative question.

Practice: 1. Repair `She said me that it was ready`. 2. Report `When can you start?` beginning `She asked ...` in a past context. 3. Agree with `I haven't checked it` using neither. 4. A colleague promised to send a report, but delivery is unverified. Repair `The report has been sent` to preserve only the known fact.

<details>
<summary>Section 12 answers</summary>

1. She told me that it was ready. / She said to me that it was ready. 2. She asked when I could start. 3. Neither have I. 4. The colleague said they would send the report, but delivery has not been confirmed.

</details>

Transfer: a partner gives a time-specific plan and then changes it. Report the latest plan to a third person without inventing completion. Follow with two natural short replies using the correct auxiliary.

## Review without memorizing a rule list

There are 48 guided questions, plus 12 transfer tasks. Revisit errors the next day, a week later, and in a new scenario; adjust intervals to actual recall. Choose three weak topics, write one daily-life message and one work message for each, then say the messages without reading. Ask the listener to report back the action, time, owner, and uncertainty. Check meaning first, then form.

Once a common pattern works in a changed situation, continue the main course. The [optional page](07-optional-grammar.md) is for recognition, not an entrance requirement or a new memorization list.
