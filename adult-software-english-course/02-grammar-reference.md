# Grammar Reference: Build a Message, Then Check It

## How to use this page

Do not memorize the whole page before speaking. Find the meaning you want to express, read one pattern, say your own example, and return to the corresponding unit. Grammar helps listeners identify who, what, when, and whether something is true. The examples below are basic working patterns, not an exhaustive grammar of English.

If you understand rules but cannot build sentences, use the [foundation workbook: 10 stations and 68 exercises](08-foundation-workbook.md). Follow the main lessons through states versus actions, quantity, questions and negatives, common time expressions, requests, and conditions. Each station includes answers, spoken tasks, and next-day retrieval; memorizing this reference is not required.

For fuller comparisons and guided practice, use [Common grammar: 12 topics and 48 exercises](06-everyday-grammar.md). It expands sentence jobs, noun phrases, agreement, modifiers, questions, tense contrasts, passive voice, nonfinite forms, clauses, conditions, modals, and reporting. [Optional grammar](07-optional-grammar.md) gives short explanations of lower-priority patterns; you may skip those without delaying the main course.

## 1. Build the sentence skeleton: Units 1-4

| Meaning | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity or state | subject + be + description | I am a developer. The page is ready. |
| An action | subject + verb | The app runs. |
| Action on something | subject + verb + object | I check the page. |
| Give something to someone | subject + verb + object + to + person | I sent the report to Jo. |
| Make something a certain way | subject + verb + object + description | The change makes the page faster. |

The subject tells who or what; the verb expresses action or state; the object receives the action here. `I the page check` is not the ordinary English order. `I am check the page` incorrectly combines be with a base action verb. Use `I check` for a routine or `I am checking` for an activity in progress.

A sentence usually needs a subject, even where Chinese could omit it: `It is ready`, not just `Is ready` as a basic statement. Instructions can omit you: `Open the page.` Short conversational answers such as `At nine` are also natural when the question supplies the missing context.

## 2. Choose the person and ownership: Units 1-2

| Subject: does the action | Object: receives it | Before a noun: whose | Alone: whose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | me | my | mine |
| you | you | your | yours |
| he | him | his | his |
| she | her | her | hers |
| it | it | its | usually avoid a standalone form |
| we | us | our | ours |
| they | them | their | theirs |

`She sent me her report. The report is hers.` Do not use `I` after to: `Send it to me`. Singular they can refer to a person whose gender is unknown or who uses they: `The reviewer says they are ready.` Agreement remains `they are`, not `they is`.

`its` shows possession; `it's` means it is or it has: `The app has its own settings. It's ready.` `Jo's laptop` shows Jo owns it. Use `this/that` for one thing and `these/those` for several; this/these are usually nearer, that/those farther or previously mentioned.

## 3. Name things: Units 2 and 6

Countable nouns: `a file`, `two files`. A singular countable noun usually needs a determiner: `a file`, `the file`, `my file`, `this file`, not `I opened file` in ordinary prose. Uncountable nouns in common usage: `water`, `information`, `advice`, `software`, `work`. Say `some information`, `a piece of advice`, `two tasks`, not `two informations` or `many works` when meaning tasks.

Use a/an for one non-specific thing, choosing by the next sound: `a user` starts with a /j/ sound; `an error` starts with a vowel sound. Use the for something identified in context: `I found a bug. The bug affects login.` General plurals can omit an article: `Developers write code.`

Common plurals: file/files; box/boxes; company/companies; person/people; child/children. An adjective does not become plural: `two new files`.

`some` and `any`: `I have some questions`; `Do you have any questions?`; `I don't have any questions`. Requests can use some: `Could I have some water?` `many` with countable plurals, `much` with uncountables, `a lot of` with either. `a few tests` means some; `few tests` emphasizes not many. `a little time` means some; `little time` emphasizes shortage.

## 4. Questions and negatives: Units 4-5

| Main construction | Statement | Question | Negative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| be | She is ready. | Is she ready? | She isn't ready. |
| Ordinary present verb | She works here. | Does she work here? | She doesn't work here. |
| Ordinary past verb | She tested it. | Did she test it? | She didn't test it. |
| Modal | She can help. | Can she help? | She can't help. |
| Present perfect | She has finished. | Has she finished? | She hasn't finished. |

After do/does/did use the base verb. Do not add do where another helper already makes the question: not `Do you are ready?` Add a question word before the question pattern: `Where does she work?` If who is the subject, use `Who owns this task?`, not `Who does own ...?` for a neutral question.

Polite embedded question: `Could you tell me where the file is?` The inner part is statement order, not `where is the file`. `Could you repeat that?` requests an action; `What does that mean?` requests meaning.

Short answers preserve the helper: `Yes, I am`; `No, she doesn't`; `Yes, we have`. `Yes` alone can be ambiguous after a negative question. Prefer an explicit fact: `The test has passed` or `It has not passed yet`.

## 5. Time, place, and routine: Units 3-4 and 7

Time: `at nine`, `on Monday`, `in July`, `in 2027`; no preposition before `today`, `tomorrow`, `last week`, or `next week` in ordinary time phrases. `by Friday` sets a deadline; `on Friday` names the day; `until Friday` continues a situation to that time. Compare `I'll send it by Friday` and `I'll stay here until Friday`.

Place: `in the office`, `on the desk`, `at the entrance`, `next to the lift`, `between the door and the window`. Different prepositions can express different viewpoints; learn common phrases in context rather than translating one Chinese word mechanically.

Existence: `There is a problem`; `There are two problems`; `Is there a problem?` Possession: `I have a problem`, not `There have a problem`.

Frequency: `I usually check first`; `She is usually ready`; `I don't usually work on Sunday`. Be clear whether the activity or the frequency is being negated.

## 6. Choose the time and status: Units 8-11

| Meaning | Form | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Habit or general fact | base verb / third-person s | She tests every day. |
| Current/temporary activity | am/is/are + ing | She is testing now. |
| Finished past event | past form | She tested it yesterday. |
| Past activity in progress | was/were + ing | She was testing when I called. |
| Plan/intention | am/is/are going to + base | She is going to test tomorrow. |
| Decision/offer/promise/prediction | will + base | I'll send an update. |
| Arranged future event | present continuous + future context | We're meeting on Friday. |
| Present completion/experience | have/has + participle | She has finished. Have you used it? |

Past continuous is an optional extension of Unit 9: it provides background around another past event. It does not mean every long activity requires a continuous tense.

Use past simple with a finished past-time label: `I sent it yesterday`. Use present perfect for the current completion question: `I've sent it`; then answer `When?` with past simple. `I've worked here for two years` describes a period continuing to now; `for` gives duration, `since` gives a starting point: `since 2025`.

`already` often marks completion, `yet` an expected completion in questions/negatives, and `still` continuation. None proves the tense on its own. `I still work here` and `I'm still working on it` mean different things.

Many state meanings use simple forms: `I know`, `I need`, `I understand`. Future forms overlap; a will sentence is not automatically more certain than a going-to sentence. Add explicit uncertainty when needed: `I expect to finish on Friday, but access is not confirmed`.

## 7. Useful irregular verb forms

| Base | Past | Past participle | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| be | was/were | been | It has been slow today. |
| have | had | had | We had a meeting. |
| do | did | done | I've done the check. |
| go | went | gone | She went home yesterday. |
| get | got | got/gotten | I've got access now. |
| make | made | made | We made a change. |
| take | took | taken | It took ten minutes. |
| find | found | found | I've found the error. |
| send | sent | sent | I sent the report. |
| write | wrote | written | I've written the notes. |
| read | read | read | I read the report yesterday. |
| run | ran | run | The test has run twice. |
| say | said | said | She said it was ready. |
| tell | told | told | He told me the time. |

Present read is /riːd/; past/participle read is /red/. Got/gotten usage varies by dialect and meaning. Learn the sentence you need instead of treating variants as errors automatically.

## 8. Requests, obligations, and possibilities: Units 10-16

Modals take the base verb: `can help`, `could check`, `should test`, `must stop`, `might fail`, `will send`; no third-person s and no to after these modals. `have to` and `need to` use to: `She has to check`; `She needs to check`.

| Intention | Example | Caution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ability/permission | I can help. Can I use this? | Clarify whose permission is needed in real work |
| Polite request | Could you check the time? | Does not necessarily refer to past time |
| Advice | We should add a test. | Label mandatory review changes explicitly |
| Requirement | We must keep the title. | Not a claim that it is already implemented |
| Prohibition | You must not share the token. | Means forbidden |
| No obligation | You don't have to reply today. | Does not forbid a reply |
| Possibility | It might be a network issue. | Not a confirmed cause |

## 9. Join ideas: Units 12, 15, and 17

`and` adds; `but` contrasts; `because` gives a reason; `so` gives a result; `although` introduces a contrast despite something. `Although the test passed, we need approval.` Avoid paired `although ... but` or `because ... so` in these standard basic patterns.

Real future condition: `If the test passes, we can release.` This is conditional, not permission to skip approval. The if-clause uses present, not will in this ordinary prediction pattern. Future time clauses work similarly: `I'll send the report when it is ready`.

Compare: `faster than`, `easier than`, `more reliable than`, `better than`. Equality: `as fast as`. Highest within a stated group: `the fastest of these three options`. State the comparison and evidence instead of calling something best without a criterion.

Embedded content: `I think the test is complete`; `We need to know whether the test passed`. For statements about uncertainty, `whether` means "whether or not," not the time word `when`.

## 10. Combine actions and descriptions: Units 16 and 18

Learn verb combinations as phrases: `want to check`, `need to check`, `plan to check`, `enjoy reading`, `avoid sending`, `suggest adding`. `let users upload` but `allow users to upload`. Do not assume every second verb takes the same form.

Purpose: `Open the page to check the result`. Relative clause: `The test that failed is for large files.` The that-part identifies which test. For speech, split a difficult sentence: `One test failed. It checks large files.`

## 11. Passive and reporting: Units 17 and 19

Passive = be + participle: `The bug was reported yesterday`; `The tests are run daily`; `The change has been approved`. It highlights what happened to the subject. Active = a doer performs an action: `Eva approved the change`. Prefer active when responsibility matters.

Be + adjective may instead describe a state: `The page is ready`. Do not label every sentence with be as passive. `The page is loading` is continuous, not passive.

Reported speech: `Jo said, "I am busy"` can become `Jo said they were busy`. Pronouns and time words depend on who reports and when. If the statement is still true, retaining present can be appropriate: `Jo says they are busy`. Keep the source and facts clear rather than changing words mechanically.

## 12. Writing and repair practice

Use a capital at the beginning, `I` capitalized, and a period/question mark at the end. Join full clauses with appropriate punctuation or a connector: `The test failed, so we stopped`; not a long chain of comma-only sentences. In work messages, headings and lists are useful, but each status must still be unambiguous.

Repair these before opening the answers:

1. She don't have two informations.
2. Is you work here?
3. I am test it yesterday.
4. Have you wrote the report yet?
5. I will to send it when it will be ready.
6. Could you explain why did it fail?
7. It is more easier because it has fewer steps, so we chose it.
8. I suggest to add a test. The change was approve.
9. Users mustn't add a comment. (Intended meaning: a comment is optional.)
10. The tests passed. Therefore the release is approved. (No approval evidence is supplied.)
