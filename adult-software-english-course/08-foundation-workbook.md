# Foundation Workbook: Make Everyday Sentences Reliable

[中文](08-foundation-workbook.zh.md) · [Course](README.md) · [Grammar reference](02-grammar-reference.md) · [Common grammar next](06-everyday-grammar.md)

## Route and Completion Checks

This workbook breaks essential sentences into 10 stations with six exercises each, followed by eight mixed checks: 68 exercises with answers. It supplements the lessons, not pronunciation, vocabulary, listening, or interaction. Start speaking before finishing it. It is not an exhaustive grammar book.

| Alongside lessons | Stations | First practical target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Units 1-3 | 1-2: states, people, quantities | Introduce people and objects; distinguish one from several |
| Units 4-7 | 3-4: routines, questions and negatives | Ask for information without translating word by word |
| Units 8-9 | 5-6: now and the past | Separate routines, ongoing activity, and finished events |
| Units 10-11 | 7-8: plans and completion | Separate intention, activity, completion, and non-completion |
| Unit 12 onward | 9-10: requests, reasons and conditions | Preserve obligation, permission, uncertainty, and dependencies |

Use at least two sessions per station: first understand and answer; later cover the models and do questions, listening contrasts, and short writing. Work on one distinction at a time. Taking several days is fine. Check unfamiliar vocabulary first; not every difficulty is a grammar error.

**Sequence: understand meaning, choose or complete, repair or ask, produce your own message, retrieve it another day.** Technical labels are optional; identifying who, what, when, and whether something is true is not.

Answer the six exercises independently. Accept natural alternatives that preserve the intended meaning. A suggested learning check is five correct exercises out of six, with no reversed time, negative, or person; then produce three original sentences without the model and answer two related questions. Revisit weak points instead of restarting everything. This is not a CEFR or formal examination standard.

These new sentences have no bundled audio. For listening contrasts, ask a partner to read only the English, or prepare both sentences with TTS and close the text before listening. Previously read material is supported practice. Directly playable recordings are in the [listening lab](04-listening-lab.md).

## Station 1: Separate States from Actions

Alongside Units 1-2. The subject is the person or thing this sentence is about. Start with I, you, she, we, they, and it. The verb be has different forms; am does not belong before every English verb.

| Meaning | Example | Pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Identity | I am a developer. | I + am + identity |
| State | She is busy. | she/he/it + is + description |
| Location | We are at home. | we/you/they + are + place |
| Action | I work at home. | Subject + action verb; no extra am |

I'm = I am; she's = she is; we're = we are in these examples. Learn the complete forms, then recognize the contractions. English needs is in She is busy even if your first language has no corresponding word here. I am work mixes two patterns incorrectly.

Put not after be: She is not busy / She isn't busy. To ask, move be before the subject: Is she busy? Answer Yes, she is / No, she isn't. I'm not ready is a useful negative form.

### Six Exercises

1. To describe her current state, choose She is busy / She works busy.
2. Complete: I ___ ready. They ___ at home.
3. Repair I am work here, meaning that this is your workplace.
4. Make The room is ready negative.
5. Ask whether the person you are speaking to is a teacher, using you; then give a negative short answer as that person.
6. Write two sentences: your name is Lin; you are in the office now.

<details>
<summary>Station 1 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. She is busy. busy describes a state; works busy does not express this meaning.
2. am; are. Match be to the subject.
3. I work here. Do not put am before the bare action verb.
4. The room is not ready / isn't ready. Keep is in the negative.
5. Are you a teacher? No, I'm not. The speaker answers about themselves with I.
6. My name is Lin. I'm in the office now. I'm Lin also introduces your name.

</details>

Speak: introduce a fictional colleague's name, job, and current state. Answer Are you ready? and Are you at home? truthfully. Listen: She is ready / She isn't ready; recognizing ready alone does not establish readiness. Write: send a friend two sentences about your location and state.

## Station 2: One, Several, Whose, and What Exists

Alongside Units 2-3 and 6. A file is countable: a file, two files. Water and information are normally uncountable: some water, some information. Use a unit when needed: two bottles of water.

A singular countable noun usually needs a determiner in an ordinary complete sentence: I have a laptop, not I have laptop. Choose a/an by the next sound: a user starts with the consonant /j/; an app starts with a vowel sound.

Introduce something with a/an, then use the when both people can identify it: I have a bag. The bag is black. My bag already identifies whose bag; do not say a my bag. General plural statements can omit the: Files take space.

| Distinction | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Possession | I have a charger. |
| Something exists in a place | There is a charger on the desk. |
| Several things exist | There are two chargers. |
| Possessive before a noun | This is my charger. |
| Possessive standing alone | This charger is mine. |

### Six Exercises

1. Add a/an: ___ app; ___ user.
2. Repair: I need two file and some informations.
3. Add is/are: There ___ a bag. There ___ two bags.
4. Repair: This is mine laptop.
5. Introduce one problem, then say that this same problem is small.
6. Write two sentences: you have a laptop; two books are on the desk. Use have and there are.

<details>
<summary>Station 2 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. an app; a user. Use the sound, not the letter name.
2. I need two files and some information. files is plural; information is normally uncountable.
3. is; are. Match the singular or plural noun in these sentences.
4. This is my laptop. Or This laptop is mine. my goes before the noun; mine stands alone.
5. There is a problem. The problem is small. The second sentence refers to the known problem.
6. I have a laptop. There are two books on the desk. Do not use There have.

</details>

Speak: describe the quantity, ownership, and location of three everyday objects. Answer Is this your bag? and Are there two books? Listen: There is a file / There are two files; record the quantity. Write: describe your desk in three sentences without confusing my and mine.

## Station 3: Present Simple for Routines and General Situations

Alongside Unit 4. Present simple does not mean an activity happening at this exact second. I work here describes a general situation; I check email every morning describes a routine.

Use the base form with I/you/we/they. In affirmative sentences, he/she/it or a single person's name normally takes the third-person form: I work becomes She works. Common changes: work/works, watch/watches, study/studies, have/has. Consonant + y changes to ies in study; play becomes plays instead.

| Subject | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I/you/we/they | They work here. | They don't work here. | Do they work here? |
| he/she/it | She works here. | She doesn't work here. | Does she work here? |

Does/doesn't already carries the grammatical change, so work returns to its base form. Do not write Does she works or She doesn't works.

Frequency words commonly go before ordinary verbs: I usually cook at home. They go after be: She is usually busy. Know, need, and like also follow this sentence pattern; the verb need not describe physical movement.

### Six Exercises

1. Complete with work: I ___ here. Mei ___ here.
2. Repair: He have a bike.
3. Make She checks email every morning negative.
4. Repair: Does she likes coffee?
5. Ask whether the listener cooks at home; give an affirmative short answer as the listener.
6. Use usually to describe one of your habits and one fictional colleague's habit.

<details>
<summary>Station 3 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. work; works. Mei names one person.
2. He has a bike. has is the third-person form of have.
3. She doesn't check email every morning. This denies the every-morning routine; it does not necessarily mean she never checks.
4. Does she like coffee? Use like after does.
5. Do you cook at home? Yes, I do.
6. Example: I usually walk to work. Jo usually takes the bus. Other facts are fine; check subject-verb agreement.

</details>

Speak: ask each other about a workplace and an everyday habit. Listen: She works here / She doesn't work here; record whether this is her workplace. Write: two routine sentences plus one state sentence from Station 1, such as workplace and whether you are busy today.

## Station 4: Questions with Be or Do

Alongside Unit 5. Inspect the existing verb before forming a question. Do does not go before every question.

| Information wanted | Statement to question | Key step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Readiness | You are ready → Are you ready? | Move be to the front |
| Need for help | You need help → Do you need help? | Use do for an ordinary present-simple verb |
| Her workplace | She works here → Where does she work? | Question word + does + subject + base form |
| Person responsible | Jo owns this task → Who owns this task? | Who is itself the subject; no added do |

What asks about a thing or information; where a place; when a time; why a reason; how a way or manner. How often asks frequency; how long asks duration. These are not interchangeable.

Match short answers to the auxiliary: Are you ready? Yes, I am. Do you need help? No, I don't. Start with direct questions. Learn Could you repeat that? as a useful request; longer embedded questions appear in Common Grammar Section 5.

### Six Exercises

1. Repair: Do you are available?
2. Repair: Are you need help?
3. Ask where she works, using she and work.
4. Ask which person needs a charger, using who, need, and a charger.
5. Ask the frequency of checking messages: choose How long / How often and form the complete question with you.
6. Answer Are you at home? affirmatively and Does Jo work here? negatively.

<details>
<summary>Station 4 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. Are you available? Do not add do to be.
2. Do you need help? need is an ordinary verb.
3. Where does she work? This is a direct question, not Where she works?
4. Who needs a charger? Who is the subject; a neutral question needs no do.
5. How often do you check messages? Ask frequency, not the duration of each check.
6. Yes, I am. No, Jo doesn't. Or No, they don't, using singular they for Jo. Match the verb to the chosen subject.

</details>

Speak: ask about a name, a location, and a routine without repeating one template. Listen: Where do you work? / When do you work? Identify the information needed before answering. Write: two clarification questions for a colleague; do not invent their answers.

## Station 5: Usually Doing Something versus Doing It Now

Alongside Unit 8. Present continuous needs both am/is/are and -ing: I am testing, not I testing or I am test.

| Situation | Example | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Usual situation | I work in the office. | General situation |
| Temporary situation today | I'm working at home today. | Temporary; not necessarily typing at this second |
| Current activity | The page is loading. | Ongoing, not necessarily finished |
| State | I understand the question. | Normally not am understanding |

Negative: She isn't working. Question: Is she working? Answer Yes, she is / No, she isn't. Do not add does.

Start with these spellings: work/working; use/using, dropping silent e; run/running, doubling n after a stressed short vowel in this word; see/seeing. Not every final consonant doubles. Check a dictionary for unfamiliar words instead of guessing from the last letter.

### Six Exercises

1. For an ordinary weekly routine, choose I check / I'm checking email every Monday.
2. Complete the ongoing activity: She ___ ___ (use) the laptop.
3. Repair: They testing now.
4. Repair I'm knowing the answer, meaning that you know it.
5. Ask whether the page is loading and give a negative short answer.
6. Write two sentences: your usual drink is tea; you are drinking water now.

<details>
<summary>Station 5 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. I check email every Monday. Use present simple for this ordinary recurring habit; this is not an absolute rule triggered by a time word alone.
2. is using. Use is with she and drop the e in use.
3. They are testing now. are is missing.
4. I know the answer. This meaning of know normally does not take the continuous.
5. Is the page loading? No, it isn't.
6. I usually drink tea. I'm drinking water now. Separate the habit from the current activity.

</details>

Speak: ask and answer about two nearby activities, then describe the corresponding routines. Listen: I test the page / I'm testing the page; notice I'm and -ing. Write: two current progress sentences. Do not interpret is loading as has loaded.

## Station 6: What Happened Yesterday

Alongside Unit 9. Past simple can describe events in a finished period or past states. Yesterday, last night, and an hour ago normally take no at/on/in before them.

| Type | Affirmative | Negative | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary action | I checked it. | I didn't check it. | Did you check it? |
| State with be | She was busy. | She wasn't busy. | Was she busy? |

I/he/she/it normally takes was; you/we/they takes were. After did/didn't, use the base verb, not didn't checked. Past be already has was/were; do not say Did she was busy.

Regular changes: check/checked, use/used, study/studied, stop/stopped. Start with useful irregulars: go/went, have/had, send/sent, write/wrote. You need not memorize a long list before speaking. The ending -ed is not always an extra syllable; check the pronunciation page or a dictionary.

### Six Exercises

1. Complete with be: I ___ busy yesterday. We ___ at home last night.
2. Use past send: She ___ the message yesterday.
3. Repair: I didn't went to the office.
4. Ask whether the listener checked the file yesterday.
5. Repair: Did the room was clean?
6. Write two sentences: yesterday you opened the page, then checked the settings.

<details>
<summary>Station 6 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. was; were.
2. sent.
3. I didn't go to the office. Use go after didn't.
4. Did you check the file yesterday?
5. Was the room clean? Move past be directly before the subject.
6. I opened the page yesterday. Then I checked the settings. Clear sequence does not require past perfect for every earlier action.

</details>

Speak: ask Were you busy yesterday? then What did you do? Listen: I sent it / I didn't send it; preserve the negative. Write: three sentences about yesterday's events. Simple past sentences are sufficient; length is not the goal.

## Station 7: Intentions, Immediate Decisions, and Arrangements

Alongside Unit 10. Future meaning is not limited to will. Different forms are not a ranking of certainty.

| Situation | Common expression | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Existing intention | I'm going to study tonight. | An intention for tonight |
| Decision to help now | I'll help you. | Offering help at this moment |
| Arranged event | I'm meeting Jo at ten tomorrow. | A meeting already arranged |

Going to needs be + going to + base verb: She is going to check. Keep is. Will takes the bare verb: She will check, without to or s. Going to can also predict from present evidence, and will can make predictions too; neither has only the use shown in the table.

**I'm checking the file** describes checking in progress; **I'm going to check the file** expresses an intention. The word going does not mean the checking has begun.

Negatives: I'm not going to join; I won't join. Questions: Are you going to join? / Will you join? Interpret them in context. For arrangements, include a clear future time: I'm meeting Jo tomorrow avoids an unintended reading about a meeting happening now.

### Six Exercises

1. Repair: She going to call tomorrow.
2. Repair: I will to send the message.
3. Does I'm going to test it after lunch establish that testing has already started? Choose not established / definitely started.
4. Say that you do not intend to work tonight, using going to.
5. Ask whether the listener intends to join the meeting, using going to.
6. Situation A: decide to help a colleague now. Situation B: you have arranged to meet Jo at nine tomorrow; use present continuous. Write one sentence for each.

<details>
<summary>Station 7 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. She is going to call tomorrow.
2. I will send the message. No to after will.
3. Not established. It describes an intention for after lunch.
4. I'm not going to work tonight.
5. Are you going to join the meeting?
6. A: I'll help you. B: I'm meeting Jo at nine tomorrow. Forms can overlap in real contexts; practise the specified uses here.

</details>

Speak: ask about tonight's plans and one arrangement for tomorrow. Listen: I'm checking it / I'm going to check it; record whether checking is underway. Write: separate a next-step plan from an accomplished fact. When appropriate, use I expect to ... for an expectation. Never report a plan as completion.

## Station 8: Current Completion versus a Past Event

Alongside Unit 11. Present perfect uses have/has + past participle and connects past and present: current completion, experience up to now, or a situation continuing until now. It is not the automatic translation of a completion marker in another language.

| Question being answered | Example | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What happened yesterday? | I sent it yesterday. | Specific finished past time |
| Is sending complete now? | I've sent it. | Current completion report |
| Is this part of your experience? | Have you used this app? | Experience up to now |
| How long up to now? | I've worked here for two years. | A situation continuing to the present |

Use have with I/you/we/they and has with he/she/it. Negative: I haven't sent it yet. Question: Have you sent it yet? Answers: Yes, I have / No, I haven't. The following form is a participle, not automatically past simple: write/wrote/written; send/sent/sent.

For introduces duration, such as for two hours; since introduces a starting point, such as since Monday. Decide whether the situation reaches the present before choosing the form. For does not require present perfect: I worked there for two years can describe a finished period.

### Six Exercises

1. For an ordinary report of last night's event, choose I sent / I've sent the report last night.
2. Complete a current completion report: She ___ ___ (finish) the check.
3. Repair: Have you wrote the message?
4. Say that checking is not complete, using yet and check it.
5. Add for/since: I've worked here ___ March; I've worked here ___ six months.
6. Read I haven't tested it yet. I'm going to test it tomorrow. Explain the established fact and the plan in your own words or first language.

<details>
<summary>Station 8 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. I sent the report last night. Use past simple with this definite finished time.
2. has finished. The past participle of finish is finished.
3. Have you written the message? Use written after have.
4. I haven't checked it yet.
5. since; for.
6. Testing has not happened yet; tomorrow's testing is an intention. Neither passing nor completion is established.

</details>

Speak: answer Have you sent it? then When did you send it? If you have not sent it, give the plan rather than inventing a time. Listen: I've checked it / I haven't checked it. Write: one completed item, one unfinished item, and one next step. Study perfect continuous later, once these distinctions are stable.

## Station 9: Ability, Requests, Requirements, and No Obligation

Alongside Units 10 and 13-16. Can, could, should, must, and might take the base verb: She can help, not cans help, can helps, or can to help. They can also take be: It can be slow.

| Meaning | Example | Important distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ability | I can help. | Does not establish completed work |
| Request | Could you check it? | Could does not mean past time here |
| Suggestion | We should add a test. | State mandatory requirements explicitly |
| Prohibition | You mustn't share this password. | Not an optional action |
| No obligation | You don't have to reply today. | Replying is not prohibited |
| Possibility | It might be a network issue. | Not an established cause |

Have to and need to are useful but behave differently: She has to check; She doesn't have to check; Does she have to check? These express obligation, no obligation, and a question about obligation. Do not use does she must.

### Six Exercises

1. Repair: She can helps me.
2. Request repetition politely, using could and repeat that.
3. Replying tomorrow is acceptable: choose mustn't / don't have to reply today.
4. Repair: Does she has to join?
5. Evidence is insufficient. Change It is a network issue using might and say it is unconfirmed.
6. Write two sentences: suggest checking first; require checking before sending. Use should and must to distinguish them.

<details>
<summary>Station 9 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. She can help me. Use the base form after can.
2. Could you repeat that?
3. You don't have to reply today. You can reply tomorrow. No obligation is not prohibition.
4. Does she have to join? Use have after does.
5. It might be a network issue, but we haven't confirmed it. Preserve uncertainty.
6. We should check first. We must check before sending. Shorter sentences are also acceptable if the distinction is preserved.

</details>

Speak: request help, make a suggestion, and describe an optional action. Have a partner explain which actions are required. Listen: You mustn't send it / You don't have to send it. Write: one required action, one suggestion, and one unconfirmed possible cause.

## Station 10: Reasons and Conditions in Clear Short Sentences

Alongside Units 12 and 15. First make two complete short sentences, then choose their relationship. Longer is not automatically more accurate.

| Relationship | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Addition with and | I opened the page and checked the title. |
| Contrast with but | The page opens, but the image is missing. |
| Reason with because | I can't join because I'm busy. |
| Result with so | I'm busy, so I can't join. |
| Future condition with if | If I get access, I'll run the test. |
| Future time with when | I'll call when I finish. |

For these basic standard patterns, do not combine because ... so or although ... but. Use because to introduce the cause, or reorder the information and use so for the result. Because normally takes a clause; because of takes a noun phrase: because I'm busy / because of the meeting.

In these real future conditions and time clauses, use present simple after if/when; the main clause can use will: If it works, I'll use it. This is not an absolute ban on will in every sentence containing if. Practise this use first; learn embedded if/whether meaning an unknown yes/no answer later.

### Six Exercises

1. Choose the contrast: The file is small, ___ it takes a long time to open. (but / because)
2. Join I can't join. I'm busy with because, preserving being busy as the reason.
3. Repair: Because I'm tired, so I'll rest.
4. Repair: If I will get access, I'll check it.
5. Repair: We stopped because of the test failed.
6. Testing passed, but approval has not arrived. Write one sentence with but; do not claim release is permitted. Use approval and pending if helpful.

<details>
<summary>Station 10 Answers and Reasons</summary>

1. but, expressing contrast.
2. I can't join because I'm busy.
3. Because I'm tired, I'll rest. Or I'm tired, so I'll rest.
4. If I get access, I'll check it. No will in this use of the if-clause.
5. We stopped because the test failed. Or We stopped because of the failed test. Clause versus noun phrase.
6. The test passed, but approval is still pending. Passing is not approval.

</details>

Speak: propose a plan and answer Why? and What if you don't get access? Short answers are fine. Listen: I'll send it / I'll send it if it is approved; note the condition. Write: a four-sentence update including a known fact, a problem, a next step, and a condition.

## Mixed Check: Without Sentence Frames

Try this after Station 10, then a week later with changed names, objects, and times. These eight exercises mix earlier topics; the most recently studied form is not automatically the answer.

1. Repair: She is work here every day, but today she work at home.
2. Repair: There have two new file on my desk.
3. Ask Sam directly, using you, whether Sam sent the message yesterday. Sam did not; answer as Sam.
4. Repair: I have wrote the report yesterday.
5. Someone asks Are you testing now? You have not started and intend to test after lunch. Answer without claiming current activity.
6. A comment was intended to be optional, but the message says You mustn't add a comment. Repair the meaning.
7. Use if: getting access tomorrow is the condition for checking the file; we will check if access arrives.
8. Write four or five sentences from these facts: you checked the page yesterday; you are checking images today; the report is not sent; you intend to send it tomorrow; permission to release is unconfirmed. Do not invent a fix or approval.

<details>
<summary>Mixed Check Model Answers</summary>

1. She works here every day, but today she is working at home. Routine versus temporary situation.
2. There are two new files on my desk. Existence, plural noun, and no plural s on the adjective.
3. Did you send the message yesterday? No, I didn't. Match the question and answer with did.
4. I wrote the report yesterday. For a current completion report, I've written the report works, but not with yesterday retained here.
5. No, I'm not. I'm going to test after lunch. Not underway; intended for later.
6. You don't have to add a comment. Or Adding a comment is optional. No obligation, not prohibition.
7. If we get access tomorrow, we'll check the file.
8. I checked the page yesterday. I'm checking the images today. I haven't sent the report yet. I'm going to send it tomorrow. We haven't confirmed whether we can release. The final sentence can also be We don't know yet if we can release. Here whether/if embeds an unknown yes/no answer; see Common Grammar Section 9 as needed.

</details>

## Review and Next Steps

| When | Action | Record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same day | Answer, check reasons, and say corrected sentences | Person, time, negative, quantity, or form error |
| Next day | Retrieve missed answers and change one fact | Can you build the sentence independently? |
| Three days later | Mix two stations and ask two questions | Are you choosing from meaning or guessing from the preceding item? |
| One week later | Write an update and answer spoken follow-up questions | Can you preserve facts without memorizing a model? |

A suggested mixed-check target is seven usable answers out of eight, with no reversed time, negative, or fact, before adding more grammar load. Judge open answers by factual completeness and broadly correct form, not exact wording. If needed, keep doing manageable communication tasks while revisiting weak stations; do not restart the entire course.

Next use Common Grammar for articles and quantity, pronouns and agreement, comparison, and prepositions (Sections 1-4), then direct and embedded questions (Section 5). Alongside Units 13-20, practise common passives, combinations such as want to / enjoy doing, basic clauses, and reported messages (Sections 7-9 and 12). Split perfect continuous, past counterfactuals, and past deductions in Sections 6, 10, and 11 into later sessions as needed. The label common does not mean memorize everything at once. Formal inversion and similar structures remain optional.

Follow the existing listening route: Labs 1-5 after Units 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10; Labs 6-10 after Units 12, 14, 16, 18, and 20. Answer meaning questions before examining one grammatical pattern. Listening should not become only tense identification. Passing foundation checks does not guarantee comprehension of unfamiliar accents; continue human listening and interaction.
