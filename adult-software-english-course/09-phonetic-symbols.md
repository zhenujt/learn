## 2. Verified long-word method from Lesson 1

The Lesson 1 description says, when you see a long word:

1. Find the vowel letters.
2. Cut from the back, one vowel with one consonant.
3. Use the ending to judge stress.
4. Blend slowly, then speed up.

It uses `maneuverability` and this teacher tip:

- Write the consonants first: `m n v r b l t`
- The letters `eu` are read `[u]`
- The letter `i` is read `[ɪ]`
- Remaining vowel symbols are `[ə]`
- Full transcription: `[məˌnuvrəˈbɪlətɪ]`

That transcription shows the convention:

| Feature | Lesson 1 writing | Do not mix with a British course |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Brackets | Square brackets `[ ]` | Follow the video with `[]`; slashes are another common convention |
| Length mark | No `ː` | Do not rewrite `[i]` as `/iː/` before matching her board |
| American r and stress | Marks `ˈ` and `ˌ`; rhotic habits appear in words | Do not force non-rhotic British `/ɜː/` onto bird |
| Sounds in go / name | KK lessons often write `[o]` and `[e]` | British lessons often write `/əʊ/` and `/eɪ/` |

The description also says that whether you use phonics or KK, you must first be able to say the word correctly. Phonics is a spelling cue; KK is transcription. Read the word first, then use the symbols to remember it.

## 3. Complete KK chart for following the lessons

KK (Kenyon–Knott) mainly records **American** pronunciation. The table below lists common KK teaching symbols, example words, and production cues. Counts vary by textbook: a core set is about **14 monophthongs + 3 diphthongs + 24 consonants**; some charts add `[ju]` and `[ts dz tr dr]`. This is not the full IPA, and not a British 20-vowel table.

Written cues are only movement hints. While watching Chou Yu-Ju, follow her mouth shape and recording.

### 3.1 Monophthongs

| KK | Example words | Beginner cue | Common British writing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [i] | see; me | High front tongue; KK usually omits a length mark | /iː/ |
| [ɪ] | sit; big | Laxer and slightly lower than [i]; not merely a shortened [i] | /ɪ/ |
| [e] | name; day | KK often writes this glide as [e]; it is not the bed vowel | /eɪ/ |
| [ɛ] | bed; ten | Moderate opening; do not treat [e] and [ɛ] as one symbol | /e/ or /ɛ/ |
| [æ] | cat; apple | More open than [ɛ]; not the letter name A | /æ/ |
| [ɑ] | hot; father | American often writes this open back vowel as [ɑ] | British hot often /ɒ/; father often /ɑː/ |
| [ɔ] | law; saw | Back and rounded; some American accents merge it with [ɑ] | /ɔː/ |
| [o] | go; home | KK often writes [o]; not the sound of every written O | /əʊ/ or /oʊ/ |
| [ʊ] | book; good | Lax high back vowel; not merely shortened [u] | /ʊ/ |
| [u] | food; blue | High and usually rounded; KK usually omits a length mark | /uː/ |
| [ʌ] | cup; but | Stressed, relatively open, unrounded | /ʌ/ |
| [ə] | about; sofa | Unstressed and relaxed; Lesson 1 uses it heavily in the long word | /ə/ |
| [ɝ] | bird; work | Stressed r-colored vowel | Non-rhotic British often /ɜː/ |
| [ɚ] | teacher; water | Unstressed r-colored vowel | British often /ə/ without a final r |

### 3.2 Diphthongs

KK teaching charts usually list these three separately; [e] and [o] are already in the table above.

| KK | Example words | Beginner cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [aɪ] | time; my | Glide from an open position toward [ɪ] in one syllable |
| [aʊ] | now; house | Glide from an open position toward rounded [ʊ] |
| [ɔɪ] | boy; choice | Glide from an [ɔ]-like position toward [ɪ] |

Some charts also list [ju] (you, use). It is often [j] plus [u], not necessarily an extra basic vowel.

## 4. Consonants: 24 core entries

KK consonant symbols are largely the same as common IPA. Voiceless sounds normally lack vocal-fold vibration; voiced sounds normally have it. A letter name is not its consonant: the name B is [bi], not the consonant [b].

### 4.1 Stops

| KK | Example words | Beginner cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [p] | pen; cap | Close and release both lips; often aspirated at the start of a stressed syllable |
| [b] | bag; job | Same broad lip closure, voiced |
| [t] | ten; cat | Closure at the alveolar ridge behind the upper teeth, then release |
| [d] | day; bad | Similar place to [t], voiced |
| [k] | key; back | Back of the tongue near the soft palate, then release |
| [g] | go; bag | Similar place to [k], voiced |

### 4.2 Fricatives

| KK | Example words | Beginner cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [f] | fine; leaf | Upper teeth near the lower lip, voiceless |
| [v] | very; save | Similar contact to [f], with vibration; not [w] |
| [θ] | think; both | Tongue tip lightly near the upper teeth or between the teeth; do not bite tightly |
| [ð] | this; mother | Similar place to [θ], voiced |
| [s] | see; bus | Narrow passage near the alveolar ridge, voiceless |
| [z] | zoo; busy | Similar place to [s], voiced |
| [ʃ] | she; fish | Farther back than [s]; lips may round slightly |
| [ʒ] | vision; measure | Similar place to [ʃ], voiced; not [dʒ] |
| [h] | he; help | Air through the glottis; the mouth prepares for the next vowel |

### 4.3 Affricates, nasals, approximants

| KK | Example words | Beginner cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [tʃ] | chair; watch | Closure then friction, tightly one sound |
| [dʒ] | job; page | Similar to [tʃ], voiced; some writing uses [ʤ] |
| [m] | me; team | Lips closed; sound through the nose |
| [n] | no; ten | Tongue tip at the alveolar ridge; nasal airflow |
| [ŋ] | sing; long | Back-of-tongue closure; standard sing has no extra final [g] |
| [l] | light; feel | Air around the sides of the tongue; often darker finally |
| [r] | red; right | American approximant, not a tap series; KK bird more often uses [ɝ] |
| [w] | we; work | Rounded lips moving quickly into a vowel |
| [j] | yes; you | High front tongue gliding on; not the [dʒ] of the letter J. A few older charts write [y] |

### 4.4 Combinations some teaching charts list separately

| Combination | Example words | How to treat it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [ts] | cats; gets | [t] plus [s]; often extra in a 48-symbol chart, not usually an extra phoneme |
| [dz] | beds; adds | [d] plus [z] |
| [tr] | tree; try | [t] plus [r], often affricated in speech |
| [dr] | dream; drive | [d] plus [r] |

Whether these four count in “all symbols” depends on the chart in that lesson. If she does not list them separately, practice them as clusters.

## 5. Symbols that clash with British courses

While following Chou Yu-Ju, **use the KK column**.

| Word | KK (follow the videos) | Common British | Easy mistake |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| see | [i] | /iː/ | Seeing `ː` and assuming KK omitted it |
| name | [e] | /eɪ/ | Reading KK [e] as bed |
| bed | [ɛ] | /e/ or /ɛ/ | The glyph [e] does not mean the same sound in both systems |
| hot | [ɑ] | /ɒ/ | Putting British rounded [ɒ] on her American model |
| go | [o] | /əʊ/ | Treating KK [o] as a pure short o |
| bird | [ɝ] | /ɜː/ | Dropping American r-coloring |
| teacher | [ɚ] | Final /ə/ without r | American endings are r-colored |
| apple | [ˈæpl] or [ˈæpəl] | /ˈæpəl/ | Watch the stress mark; letter A is not [e] |

## 7. Split apple in KK, then a long word with the Lesson 1 method

**apple** is commonly `[ˈæpl]` or `[ˈæpəl]` in KK.

1. `ˈ` marks first-syllable stress; it is not an extra sound.
2. `[æ]` is the cat vowel, not the letter name [e].
3. `[p]` closes and releases the lips.
4. `[l]` or `[əl]` is light at the end.
5. Put it in `I have an apple.` Use an because the word begins with a vowel sound.

**maneuverability**, from the Lesson 1 description:

1. Consonant skeleton: `m n v r b l t`
2. `eu` → `[u]`
3. Letter `i` → `[ɪ]`
4. Remaining vowels → `[ə]`
5. Transcription: `[məˌnuvrəˈbɪlətɪ]`
6. Blend slowly, then faster. The description says many long words can be cut with syllable rules rather than memorized as one block of letters.

That rule is a study strategy from the description. It does not guarantee every English word cuts this way. Irregular words still need a model recording.
