# Sounds, Spelling, Numbers, and Clear Speech

[中文](01-sounds-and-spelling.zh.md) · [Course](README.md) · [Listening Lab](04-listening-lab.md)

## Start with sound, not a symbol exam

A letter name is used for spelling; a sound is used inside a word. The letter A is named /eɪ/, but its sound differs in `name`, `cat`, and `about`. English spelling is not perfectly regular. Hear a word, connect it to meaning and spelling, then say it. Phonetic symbols below are optional reference labels, not prerequisites.

Use an audio dictionary or a competent reader for the examples. This page contains no audio recordings. Start with one consistent pronunciation model; later listen to other accents. Both major British and American pronunciations are legitimate. Some vowel categories and /r/ vary by accent.

## Alphabet names for spelling aloud

| Letters | Names in IPA |
| --- | --- |
| A B C D E F G | /eɪ/ /biː/ /siː/ /diː/ /iː/ /ef/ /dʒiː/ |
| H I J K L M N | /eɪtʃ/ /aɪ/ /dʒeɪ/ /keɪ/ /el/ /em/ /en/ |
| O P Q R S T | /əʊ/ or /oʊ/; /piː/ /kjuː/ /ɑː/ or /ɑr/; /es/ /tiː/ |
| U V W X Y Z | /juː/ /viː/ /ˈdʌbəljuː/ /eks/ /waɪ/; /zed/ or /ziː/ |

Say `Could you spell that?` and `Is that B or P?` Practise B/P, D/T, M/N, G/J, and E/I by hearing names, not by looking at letters. For important identifiers, read back the entire string and use a team's agreed spelling convention. Do not spell real passwords aloud for practice.

Common symbols: `.` dot; `-` hyphen/dash; `_` underscore; `/` slash; `@` at. `AB-20` can be read `A, B, hyphen, two, zero`. Spell a fictional email such as `mina@example.com`; do not assume another person can infer punctuation.

## A small pronunciation map

| Contrast or feature | Example words | What to notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| /iː/ and /ɪ/ | sheep / ship | Different vowel quality as well as usual length; not simply long/short copies |
| /e/ and /æ/ | bed / bad | Open the mouth more for /æ/ in a common model |
| /uː/ and /ʊ/ | food / good | Hear different vowel quality; lips and tongue differ |
| /v/ and /w/ | vest / west | /v/: upper teeth near lower lip; /w/: rounded lips, no tooth contact |
| /θ/ and /s/ | thin / sin | /θ/: tongue tip near/between teeth with airflow; do not bite |
| /ð/ | this, they | Similar tongue position to /θ/, with voicing |
| /l/ and /r/ | light / right | /l/: tongue contact near upper teeth ridge; common English /r/ has no such contact |
| Final consonants | cap / cat / cab | Keep the word ending distinguishable; do not add an extra vowel |
| Consonant groups | next, test, script | Build slowly from the ending, then return to natural speech |
| Weak vowel /ə/ | about, support | Unstressed vowel is often short and relaxed |

Do not force the tongue or throat. The written tips are approximate; a teacher can check individual articulation. No one contrast is universally difficult for every Chinese-speaking learner. Choose targets from actual misunderstandings.

## Word endings carry meaning

Plural or third-person `s`: /s/ after most voiceless sounds (`tests`), /z/ after voiced sounds (`files`), /ɪz/ after sibilants such as /s z ʃ ʒ tʃ dʒ/ (`changes`). The last group adds a syllable. The rule depends on the final sound before the ending, not the last letter.

Past `ed`: /t/ after voiceless sounds other than /t/ (`checked`), /d/ after voiced sounds other than /d/ (`called`), /ɪd/ after /t/ or /d/ (`started`, `needed`). Do not say every ed as a separate "ed" syllable.

Exercise: group `works, runs, fixes` by /s z ɪz/; group `helped, played, wanted` by /t d ɪd/. Answers: works /s/, runs /z/, fixes /ɪz/; helped /t/, played /d/, wanted /ɪd/.

## Stress and thought groups

Word stress gives one syllable more prominence: `TAble`, `comPUter`, `deVELoper`. Capital letters here mark teaching emphasis, not normal spelling. Dictionary stress marks show syllable stress; do not infer every technical word from spelling.

In sentences, important or contrasting information often stands out: `I said FIFTEEN, not FIFTY.` Function words such as to, a, and can may be reduced in ordinary speech. Learn to recognize reductions before trying to imitate them. Do not deliberately drop negatives to sound fluent.

Practise in meaning groups: `I've finished the page / but I haven't tested it yet.` Pause at the meaning boundary, not after every word. Rising/falling intonation varies with context; not every question must rise.

## Numbers you can use immediately

| Range | Forms |
| --- | --- |
| 0-10 | zero, one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten |
| 11-19 | eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen |
| Tens | twenty, thirty, forty, fifty, sixty, seventy, eighty, ninety |
| Larger | one hundred; one hundred and five / one hundred five; one thousand |
| Mixed | twenty-one; forty-eight; ninety-nine |

In isolation, `thirTEEN` and `THIRty` commonly differ in stress, but connected speech can change prominence. Confirm digits when stakes matter. `zero` is clearer than `oh` when confusion is likely.

Time: 9:05 `nine oh five` or `nine zero five`; 14:30 `fourteen thirty` in 24-hour usage or `two thirty p.m.`. Ask about the zone. Decimal: 2.5 `two point five`; percentage: 50% `fifty percent`; price: $8.50 `eight dollars and fifty cents`. Currency and local time conventions differ.

Days: Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday. Months: January, February, March, April, May, June, July, August, September, October, November, December. A written date such as `6 July 2027` is clearer internationally than ambiguous `6/7/27`. It can be spoken `the sixth of July, twenty twenty-seven` or `July sixth, twenty twenty-seven`.

Useful ordinals: first, second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth, ninth, tenth, fifteenth, twentieth, twenty-first. A room number is usually an identifier, not an ordinal: Room 204 can be `room two oh four`.

## Ten-minute practice loop

1. Choose two contrasting words that caused confusion. Hear each from a reliable model and connect it to meaning.
2. Have a partner say six items in mixed order while you point or write. Solo: use a pre-shuffled TTS list without watching it.
3. Say each in a short sentence and record. Compare endings, stress, and intelligibility, not waveform appearance.
4. Ask a listener which word they heard. Change the sentence context so they cannot only guess from memorization.
5. Revisit the contrast tomorrow within a unit dialogue.

Spelling task: reader uses `B-15`, `P-50`, `M-30`, `N-13`; learner writes and reads back. Time task: reader uses 9:15, 9:50, 14:30 in shuffled order. The reader's actual order is the answer key; do not pre-show it to the learner.

## Check

Can someone write your fictional identifier correctly? Can you recognize a negative, final plural, and time in a short sentence? If not, train that small contrast and continue meaning-focused lessons. Use [Listening Lab](04-listening-lab.md) to move from isolated sounds to messages.
