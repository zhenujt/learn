## You may skip this whole page

These structures are lower priority for this beginner speaking route, not necessarily rare in all English. Some occur regularly in technical writing, planning, or formal meetings. Read a description when you encounter the pattern; return to active practice only when it serves a real need. There is no quiz or memorization requirement here. Common short forms such as `So do I`, ordinary passive voice, and practical hypothetical conditions remain in the common-grammar page.

## 1. Future perfect and future perfect continuous: optional for now

`will have + past participle` looks back from a future point: `By Friday, we will have completed the review`. The review is expected to be complete by then. Simpler: `We expect to finish the review by Friday`. Preserve whether it is an estimate or commitment; a tense cannot create certainty.

`will have been + -ing` measures activity up to a future point: `By noon, we will have been testing for three hours`. Simpler: `We started at nine. At noon, that will be three hours of testing`. This is about duration, not a claim that all tests will pass.

## 2. Past perfect continuous: optional for now

`had been + -ing` describes activity continuing up to a past point: `We had been testing for an hour when the power failed`. Testing started before that failure. Simpler: `We started testing an hour before the power failed`. Learn it actively if you often describe long incident timelines; ordinary past and past perfect cover many early tasks.

## 3. Mixed conditionals: optional for now

A past imagined condition can have a present imagined result: `If we had documented it, we would know the reason now`. We did not document it; the speaker imagines knowing now. This mixes times intentionally. Simpler: `We didn't document it, so we don't know the reason now`. That simpler version asserts the causal link; use it only if the evidence supports it. Do not mechanically match every if-clause to the same time in the result.

## 4. Formal subjunctive: recognize it; active mastery optional

`We recommend that each change be reviewed` uses the base form be after a recommendation, even with singular change. `It is essential that the owner approve it` similarly uses approve, not approves. Such forms are common in some formal varieties and policies. Other varieties use `should be reviewed` or `should approve`.

An everyday alternative is `We recommend reviewing each change`; for the requirement, `The owner must approve it`. Keep recommendation and obligation distinct rather than changing should to must automatically.

## 5. Formal inversion: optional for now

Fronted negative or limiting expressions can trigger auxiliary-before-subject order: `Only then did we notice the error` = `We noticed the error only then`. `Never have I seen this before` = `I have never seen this before`. `Had we known, we would have waited` = `If we had known, we would have waited`.

These are useful to recognize in writing or emphatic speech. Normal questions and `So do I` are common and are not optional just because they also involve changed order.

## 6. Cleft emphasis: optional to practise

`It was the setting that caused the failure` highlights the setting. `What we need is more evidence` highlights evidence. These are not necessarily rare in speech, but simple alternatives work at this stage: `The setting caused the failure`; `We need more evidence`. Keep the strength of the factual claim unchanged; emphasis does not prove the cause.

## 7. Absolute constructions and dense reduced clauses: optional for now

`The review complete, we started testing` has a separate subject, the review, in the opening phrase. Simpler: `After the review was complete, we started testing`. `With access restored, we could continue` means access had been restored, allowing continuation.

Technical writing also uses compact modifiers: `the data collected yesterday` = `the data that was collected yesterday` in a mass-noun reading; `the data to be reviewed` = data awaiting review. Expand these when reading. The common page teaches basic participle modifiers and subject alignment; you do not need to produce dense constructions to communicate well.

## 8. Advanced nonfinite forms: optional for now

`Having checked the logs, Jo sent an update` places checking before sending; ordinary `After Jo checked the logs, Jo sent an update` is clearer for beginners. `She seems to have missed the message` suggests an earlier missed message, not a confirmed observation. `The file needs to be checked` is an infinitive passive and is useful to recognize early; `We need to check the file` is an easy active alternative.

More layered forms such as `having been reviewed` combine earlier completion and passive meaning. Expand them into full clauses rather than learning long formula lists now.

## 9. Advanced relative links: optional for now

`The service returned three errors, two of which were timeouts` means two of the three errors were timeouts. `The person to whom I spoke` is formal; `the person I spoke to` is ordinary conversation. `Whoever owns the task should update it` means any person who owns it should do so.

You can split the first example: `The service returned three errors. Two were timeouts`. Do not skip basic who/which/that/whose; only the denser variants here are deferred.

## 10. Formal ellipsis and parallel compression: optional for now

`If necessary, restart the sample app` leaves out `it is`: `If it is necessary ...`. `Rae checked the client and Kim the server` omits the second checked. In speech, `Rae checked the client, and Kim checked the server` is easier to follow.

Keep normal short answers and parallel lists in active use: `We need to check, fix, and retest`. Defer studying every kind of omitted clause, literary ordering, and rhetorical compression. Expand missing words when meaning is unclear, and ask a real speaker to confirm rather than guessing an important instruction.

## When to return

Return when the same structure repeatedly blocks reading or when you need its meaning in conversation. First paraphrase it, then learn one example relevant to your work. Skipping these patterns now does not prevent progress through the twenty units; it also does not mean the whole of English grammar has been exhausted.
