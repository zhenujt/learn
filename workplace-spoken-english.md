# Workplace Spoken English: A Practical Guide

Chinese edition: [工作日常英语口语全指南](workplace-spoken-english.zh.md)

This guide covers everyday conversations with colleagues, managers, clients, and partner teams. It is about work, not tourism or personal-life conversations. Most sections apply across office roles; software-specific language has its own section. The aim is to communicate facts, requests, ownership, deadlines, and next steps clearly.

"Common" means broadly useful here, not a ranking from a frequency corpus. Region, company culture, relationships, delivery, and context affect how a phrase sounds.

## 1. Build a Clear Message

Use this sequence when useful: **main point → necessary context → action → timing → confirmation**. You do not need all five parts every time.

> The draft is ready. I still need the latest figures. Could you send them by 2 p.m.? Then I can finish the report today.

| Function | Reusable pattern | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Introduce the topic | I'd like to discuss the timeline. | I'd like to + base verb is a considerate opening. |
| State the situation | We're waiting for approval. | Describe the state without assigning blame. |
| Explain the reason | We need more time because the scope has changed. | Give a concrete reason. |
| Make a request | Could you review this by Thursday? | An action plus a deadline is easier to plan. |
| Give the next step | I'll update the document and send it over. | Send it over means send it to the other person. |
| Confirm understanding | Just to confirm, we're keeping the original deadline, right? | Check the agreement; do not silently decide for others. |

Contractions such as I'm, we're, I'll, we've, and don't are normal in speech. Start with complete subject-verb structures before relying on shortened replies.

## 2. Open a Conversation and Explain Responsibilities

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Do you have a minute to talk about the report? | Appropriate for a short question, not a hidden half-hour discussion. |
| Is now a good time? | Check availability before continuing. |
| I'd like to go over the plan with you. | Go over means review or discuss the details. |
| This is about the customer feedback we received yesterday. | Identify the topic quickly. |
| I'm responsible for the monthly report. | Responsible for takes a noun or an -ing form. |
| I work closely with the operations team. | Describe collaboration, not necessarily team membership. |
| Who would be the best person to ask about this? | Find the right contact without making assumptions. |
| Let me give you a bit of context. | Introduce only the background the listener needs. |

## 3. Ask for Repetition and Clarification

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Sorry, I didn't catch the last part. | Catch means hear or understand here. |
| Could you say that again a little more slowly? | More considerate than a bare What? |
| What does "sign-off" mean here? | Ask about a term; sign-off often means formal approval. |
| Could you give me an example? | Make an abstract requirement concrete. |
| Could you put that in the chat? | Useful for names, links, and numbers. |
| When you say "ready," do you mean ready for review or ready to publish? | Remove ambiguity from a key word. |
| Let me check that I've understood correctly. | Follow this with your interpretation. |
| Sorry, let me rephrase that. | Repair an unclear or incorrect statement naturally. |

I see signals understanding, not necessarily agreement. That makes sense recognizes a reasonable explanation, but does not always accept the proposal. I agree explicitly agrees. Got it is a normal brief acknowledgment; repeat the owner and deadline for important commitments.

## 4. Clarify Requirements and Scope

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| What are we trying to achieve? | Establish the purpose before discussing implementation. |
| What's the main priority? | Resolve competing demands. |
| Who is the intended audience? | Useful for reports, presentations, and products. |
| What needs to be included? | Separate mandatory content from optional content. |
| Is this part of the current scope? | Check whether a request adds work. |
| What would a successful outcome look like? | Ask for an observable result. |
| Could we agree on the acceptance criteria first? | Define how delivery will be judged. |
| Let me summarize the requirements before we start. | Give others a chance to correct misunderstandings. |

Words such as soon, simple, and ready need clarification. Ask **By when, exactly?** or **Which parts can we leave out?**

## 5. Request Help and Access

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Could you take a look at this when you have a moment? | Low-pressure request; specify a deadline if needed. |
| Could you help me understand this process? | More actionable than simply asking for help. |
| Would you mind reviewing this section? | Mind takes reviewing, not to review. |
| Could you walk me through the steps? | Ask for a step-by-step explanation. |
| I need access to the shared folder. | Access is normally uncountable and takes to. |
| Who can approve this request? | A subject question does not need does. |
| I've tried restarting it, but the issue is still there. | Explain what you have already tried. |
| Could you help me check whether I'm missing anything? | Invite a review for omissions. |

To accept Would you mind...?, say **Not at all. I'll take a look.** A bare Yes can be ambiguous. To accept Could you...?, try **Sure, I can do that by three.**

## 6. Accept, Decline, and Negotiate Workload

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Sure, I'll take care of it. | A real commitment, not just acknowledgment. |
| I can have a first draft ready by tomorrow. | Specify the delivery stage. |
| I can help with the review, but I can't take on the whole task. | State a useful, clear boundary. |
| I'm at capacity this week. | Your workload is full; this is not a claim about skill. |
| If this takes priority, which task should I put on hold? | Ask the decision-maker to resolve a priority conflict. |
| I won't be able to finish both by Friday. | State the constraint explicitly. |
| Could we move the deadline to Monday? | Request an adjustment rather than changing it unilaterally. |
| I can't commit to that date until we confirm the scope. | Commit to takes a noun or an -ing form. |

## 7. Schedule Meetings and Confirm Time Zones

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Are you available at 10 a.m. on Tuesday? | At for a clock time; on for a day. |
| What time works best for you? | Works means is suitable. |
| Could we set aside half an hour for this? | Specify the expected duration. |
| I'll send a calendar invite. | Invite is a common informal noun at work. |
| Could we move the meeting from Tuesday to Wednesday? | Less ambiguous than move it forward. |
| Can we start fifteen minutes later? | Both people must know the original time. |
| Is that 3 p.m. London time? | Confirm the date too; daylight saving affects offsets. |
| I have another meeting at eleven, so I'll need to leave on time. | Set expectations early. |

By Friday does not automatically mean Friday at 5 p.m. EOD and COB have no universal clock time. Prefer **Could you send it by 5 p.m. Singapore time on September 24?** Numeric dates such as 09/10 are region-dependent; name the month.

## 8. Lead and Participate in Meetings

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Shall we get started? | A familiar opening, especially in British English. |
| The goal today is to agree on the next steps. | Define the meeting outcome. |
| Let's start with a quick update. | Brief does not mean omitting important risks. |
| Could I add something here? | Ask for a turn. |
| Sorry to interrupt, but could we clarify one point? | Interrupt when clarification genuinely matters. |
| We haven't heard from the support team yet. | Invite another perspective. |
| Could we come back to the main question? | Return to the agenda. |
| Let's discuss the details after the meeting. | Clearer to learners than take it offline, which need not mean disconnecting. |

## 9. Handle Remote Calls

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Can you hear me clearly? | Check the audio. |
| I think you're on mute. | Soften a correction. |
| Your audio is cutting out. | Describe intermittent sound. |
| There's a slight delay. | Explain delayed replies or overlapping speech. |
| Could you share your screen? | Do not say share me your screen. |
| Is the text large enough to read? | Enough follows the adjective. |
| I'll rejoin the call. | Explain the next action. |
| I missed the last point because my connection dropped. | Identify what needs repeating. |

## 10. Report Progress

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| I've finished the first draft. | Present perfect emphasizes the current result. |
| I'm working on the final section now. | Present continuous describes current work. |
| We're on track to finish by Thursday. | Progress supports the date; it is not an absolute guarantee. |
| We're slightly behind schedule. | Add the reason and recovery plan. |
| The main blocker is the missing data. | Blocker is common project vocabulary. |
| I'm waiting for confirmation from finance. | Wait for, not wait someone. |
| My next step is to check the figures. | Name a concrete action. |
| There's no change since the last update. | Explicitly report an unchanged situation. |

Use **completed work → current work → blocker → next step or request**:

> I finished the draft yesterday. I'm checking the numbers now. I'm still waiting for the sales figures. If I receive them by noon, I can send the final version by four.

Finished matches yesterday; I'm checking describes current activity; the real future condition uses If I receive, not normally If I will receive.

## 11. Report Delays, Risks, and Escalations

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| There's a risk that we won't meet the deadline. | A risk is not yet a certainty. |
| The delay may affect the launch date. | May marks a possibility. |
| We need another day to complete the checks. | Another day means one additional day. |
| I wanted to flag this early. | Wanted can soften a current message. |
| Here's what we're doing to reduce the risk. | Pair the problem with action. |
| We can keep the date if we reduce the scope. | Make the condition explicit. |
| We need a decision from the project owner. | Identify the appropriate decision-maker. |
| Could you help us escalate this to the right team? | Escalate means seek a higher level of attention or handling. |

## 12. Follow Up and Remind

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Have you had a chance to review the proposal? | Ask without assuming neglect. |
| I'm following up on the approval request. | Follow up on an issue; follow up with a person. |
| Do you have an update on this? | A neutral progress question. |
| When do you think you'll be able to send it? | Request a usable estimate. |
| We need your feedback before we can move forward. | Explain the dependency. |
| Could you let me know by noon, even if it's not ready? | Ask for a status update, not necessarily delivery. |
| Just a reminder that the review is due tomorrow. | Just does not automatically make repeated reminders gentle. |
| Thanks for getting back to me. | Thank someone for replying. |

## 13. Suggest Options and Explain Trade-offs

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| One option would be to start with a smaller pilot. | Offer a possibility rather than announce a decision. |
| How about testing it with one team first? | How about takes an -ing form. |
| We could reuse the existing template. | Could can introduce a suggestion. |
| I'd recommend checking the data first. | Recommend doing is a useful pattern. |
| The advantage is that it's easier to maintain. | Explain the benefit. |
| The trade-off is that it will take longer. | Name the cost of obtaining the benefit. |
| Which option gives us the best balance of cost and quality? | Establish decision criteria. |
| Let's compare the options before we decide. | Avoid deciding too early. |

## 14. Agree, Disagree, and Express Uncertainty

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| I agree with that approach. | Agree is a verb; not I am agree. |
| That works for me. | You accept it; you are not speaking for everyone. |
| I agree with the goal, but I'm not sure about the timeline. | Separate the accepted point from the concern. |
| I see your point. My concern is the cost. | Acknowledge the reasoning, then state the issue. |
| Could we look at this from the customer's perspective? | Introduce another viewpoint. |
| I'm not convinced this will solve the underlying problem. | Express substantive doubt about the solution. |
| Based on what we know, this seems to be the best option. | Limit the claim to the available evidence. |
| I don't know yet. Let me check and get back to you by three. | Be honest and provide a next step. |

Maybe, probably, and definitely express different confidence levels. Should be ready often predicts readiness; will be ready can make a stronger commitment.

## 15. Decide, Assign Ownership, and Close

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Let's go with option B. | Use within your decision-making authority. |
| Are we all comfortable with this approach? | Invite unspoken concerns. |
| Who will take the lead on this? | Identify the lead. |
| I'll own the follow-up. | Own means take responsibility. |
| Could you confirm who's doing what? | Clarify responsibilities. |
| What are the action items? | Each item needs an action, owner, and date. |
| I'll send a summary after the meeting. | Put verbal decisions in writing. |
| Before we wrap up, is there anything we've missed? | Wrap up means finish. |

## 16. Give Feedback and Discuss Performance

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| The summary was clear and easy to follow. | Give specific praise. |
| Thanks for spotting that issue early. | Spot means notice or identify. |
| One thing we could improve is the handover process. | Focus on the process, not a personal attack. |
| Could you include an example next time? | Make feedback actionable. |
| When the update arrives late, it delays the review. | Describe behavior and impact without inventing motives. |
| How could I make these updates more useful? | Request specific feedback. |
| What should I focus on over the next month? | Align priorities and development. |
| Could we agree on what good performance looks like? | Ask for observable expectations. |

## 17. Apologize and Resolve Conflict

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| Sorry, I sent the wrong version. | State the fact directly. |
| That's my mistake. I'll correct it now. | Take responsibility and repair it. |
| I should have checked the figures first. | Should have done expresses an unfulfilled past obligation. |
| Thanks for your patience while we sort this out. | Sort out means resolve. |
| I understand why this is frustrating. | Acknowledge impact without making unsupported legal admissions. |
| Let's focus on how to prevent this from happening again. | Prevent something from happening. |
| I think we may be working with different assumptions. | Offer a testable explanation of the disagreement. |
| Can we check the facts before deciding what happened? | Avoid premature blame. |

## 18. Present Data and Speak with Clients

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| The main takeaway is that response times have improved. | Takeaway means the central conclusion. |
| Compared with last month, costs are down by ten percent. | By gives the change; to gives the resulting value. |
| These figures are estimates, not final results. | State the reliability of the information. |
| Let me show you how this works. | Introduce a demonstration. |
| Does this address the issue you raised? | Check whether the response solves the actual concern. |
| Could you tell us more about the impact on your team? | Understand the consequences before offering a fix. |
| This isn't included in the current agreement. | State the boundary, then discuss options. |
| I'll confirm what's possible before making a commitment. | Avoid promising beyond your authority. |

A rise from 20% to 25% is **five percentage points**, or a **twenty-five percent** relative increase. State the unit, interval, and baseline.

## 19. Hand Over Work and Coordinate Teams

| Phrase | Use and nuance |
| --- | --- |
| I'll be out of the office on Friday. | Explain separately whether you will be reachable. |
| Maya will cover for me while I'm away. | Agree this with Maya first. |
| Here's the current status and what's still outstanding. | Outstanding means unfinished here. |
| I've added the handover notes to the shared folder. | Tell people where the information is. |
| This depends on the legal team's approval. | Depend on, not depend of. |
| Could we align on the timeline before informing the client? | Align on means reach a shared understanding. |
| Please contact Maya if anything urgent comes up. | Come up means arise. |
| I'll catch up on the updates when I'm back. | Catch up on means review what you missed. |

## 21. Collocations, Tone, and Common Errors

### 21.1 Learn Phrases as Units

| Pattern | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| work on something | I'm working on the proposal. | Do the work. |
| work with someone | I work with the design team. | Collaborate. |
| look into something | I'll look into the issue. | Investigate. |
| look at something | Could you look at the draft? | Examine or review. |
| get back to someone | I'll get back to you tomorrow. | Reply. |
| follow up on something | I'll follow up on the request. | Check progress. |
| check with someone | Let me check with finance. | Seek confirmation. |
| keep someone posted | I'll keep you posted. | Provide continuing updates. |
| reach out to someone | I'll reach out to the owner. | Contact. |
| bring up something | Could you bring this up tomorrow? | Introduce a topic. |
| figure out something | We need to figure out why it failed. | Understand or solve. |
| run something by someone | Can I run an idea by you? | Seek an initial reaction. |

### 21.2 Please Is Not a Universal Tone Fix

| Potentially problematic wording | Clearer alternative | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Give me the report. | Could you send me the report by two? | A request usually fits ordinary collaboration better than an order. |
| You are wrong. | I think the figure may be incorrect. | Address the fact rather than label the person. |
| I want you to explain. | Could you explain how you reached that conclusion? | Ask a specific question. |
| Why didn't you finish it? | What got in the way of finishing it? | Invite information about blockers; the first can sound accusatory. |
| Please do it ASAP. | Could you finish it by 3 p.m.? | Replace vague urgency with a usable deadline. |
| Kindly do the needful. | Could you approve the request by Friday? | The first is regional; the second specifies an action internationally. |

I'll try gives neither a delivery date nor a confidence level. Prefer **I can send a draft today, but the final version will need another review.** Direct instructions can be appropriate in emergencies and clearly assigned procedures; context matters.

### 21.3 Frequent Learner Errors

| Error or less natural form | Preferred form | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I am agree. | I agree. | Agree is a verb. |
| Discuss about the plan. | Let's discuss the plan. | Discuss takes a direct object; a discussion about is fine. |
| Explain me the issue. | Could you explain the issue to me? | Explain something to someone. |
| I have a good news. | I have some good news. | News is uncountable. |
| Please reply me. | Please reply to me by email. | Reply to someone. |
| I'm boring in this meeting. | I'm bored in this meeting. | Bored is the feeling; boring describes what causes it. Consider the setting before saying either. |
| We need discuss it. | We need to discuss it. | Main-verb need takes to do. |
| I will feedback you. | I'll give you feedback tomorrow. | Give feedback is widely understood. |
| Until Friday, please send it. | Please send it by Friday. | By marks the delivery deadline. |
| Can you tell me when will it be ready? | Can you tell me when it will be ready? | Embedded questions use statement order. |

## 22. Six Model Conversations

### 22.1 Clarifying a Request

**A:** Could you prepare a short update for the client?

**B:** Sure. Should it cover only this week's progress, or the overall project status?

**A:** Just this week, including any risks.

**B:** Got it. I'll send a draft by 2 p.m. for your review.

Confirm scope and delivery stage after accepting. Should it cover...? asks what the content ought to include.

### 22.2 Asking for Focused Help

**A:** Do you have ten minutes to help me with the export?

**B:** What have you tried so far?

**A:** I've checked the filters and tried again, but the totals still don't match.

**B:** Let's compare the source data first.

Include the objective, symptom, and attempted fixes. So far often accompanies the present perfect.

### 22.3 Negotiating Deadlines

**A:** Could you finish both reports by Friday?

**B:** I can finish the sales report by then. The other one would need until Monday.

**A:** The sales report is more urgent.

**B:** Then I'll prioritize that and send the second one on Monday.

Offer a feasible alternative. Need until Monday describes the time required; send it by Monday describes a delivery deadline.

### 22.4 Disagreeing Constructively

**A:** Let's release all the changes at once.

**B:** I see the benefit, but I'm concerned about the rollback risk.

**A:** What would you suggest?

**B:** We could release to a small group first and check the error rate.

Recognize the benefit, name a specific risk, and propose an actionable alternative.

### 22.5 Updating a Client During an Incident

**A:** The report is missing yesterday's data.

**B:** Thanks for flagging that. We're checking the import now.

**A:** When will it be fixed?

**B:** I don't have a confirmed time yet. I'll send you an update by eleven, even if we're still investigating.

A promised update time is not a promised resolution time. Do not invent certainty.

### 22.6 Closing a Meeting

**A:** Before we wrap up, let's confirm the action items.

**B:** I'll update the proposal by Wednesday.

**C:** I'll review it on Thursday morning.

**A:** Great. I'll record those dates and send the summary today.

Make the action, owner, due date, and next handoff explicit.

## 23. Practice for Real Conversations

1. Pick one real scenario and five phrases per session.
2. Replace tasks, owners, and dates: report with proposal, Friday with tomorrow morning.
3. Close the guide and give a thirty-second progress, blocker, and request update.
4. Turn statements into negatives and questions; change the time reference.
5. Restate instructions: So you need the draft by Thursday, right?
6. Record yourself. Check message completeness before polishing small grammar errors.

Listen carefully for I'll versus I'd, we've versus we've been, and can versus can't. Mishearing a negative or number matters more than a non-native accent. Confirm important details. You do not need to imitate gonna or wanna, and they are not standard formal written forms.

## 24. Grammar You Need for Workplace Speech

**Start with sentence structure and everyday tenses, then requests, conditions, and clauses.** You do not need every grammatical label before speaking. However, negatives, time references, and conditions change the substance of a work commitment.

### 24.1 Sentence Parts and Five Basic Patterns [Essential]

- Subject + verb: The system crashed.
- Subject + verb + object: I updated the document.
- Subject + linking verb + complement: The document is ready.
- Subject + verb + two objects: She sent me the report.
- Subject + verb + object + complement: We need the report ready by noon.

The subject identifies who or what; the verb expresses the action or state. An object receives the action; a complement describes a subject or object. Do not omit be in The report is ready or add it to I work. For an ongoing action, use I'm working.

### 24.2 The Be, Do, and Have Systems [Essential]

| System | Statement | Negative | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Be for a state | It is ready. | It isn't ready. | Is it ready? |
| Simple present with a lexical verb | It works. | It doesn't work. | Does it work? |
| Perfect have + past participle | They have finished. | They haven't finished. | Have they finished? |

Simple-past questions use did + base verb: Did you send it?, not Did you sent it? Have can also be a lexical verb: Do you have time?

### 24.3 Simple Present and Agreement [Essential]

Use it for roles, habits, processes, and stable facts: I manage the account. The system sends a reminder every day.

Third-person singular subjects usually take -s. After does, use the base form. In The list of issues is long, list controls agreement, not issues. Everyone is here treats everyone as singular.

### 24.4 Present Continuous and State Verbs [Essential]

Use am/is/are + -ing: I'm reviewing the contract. It also describes temporary current projects: We're working with a new supplier this month.

Know, need, understand, and believe normally use simple forms for states: I understand the issue. Some verbs allow continuous forms with a different meaning: I'm thinking about the proposal describes an active process.

### 24.5 Simple Past and Past Continuous [Essential / Developing]

Use simple past for a finished past time: I sent the report yesterday. Learn useful irregular forms such as sent, made, went, found, told, and wrote.

Was/were + -ing gives an ongoing past background: I was presenting when the connection dropped. Presenting is the background; dropped is the interrupting event. This distinction helps explain incidents.

### 24.6 Present Perfect versus Simple Past [Essential]

Have/has + past participle links a past event to the present: I've sent the report emphasizes the available result. I sent it at nine places the action at a finished past time.

Common markers include already, yet, just, and so far. Have you reviewed it yet? I haven't reviewed it yet. Do not combine the ordinary present perfect with yesterday: use I sent it yesterday. American speech also commonly uses Did you send it yet?; it is not automatically an error.

### 24.7 Perfect Continuous, Since, and For [Developing]

Have/has been + -ing emphasizes an activity continuing over a period: I've been investigating this since Monday. It may still be ongoing or may just have stopped with present relevance.

Since introduces a starting point; for introduces a duration. I've worked here for three years and I've been working here for three years can both describe continuing employment, with the latter emphasizing the process. Use I have known her for years, not normally have been knowing.

### 24.8 Future Forms [Essential]

| Form | Typical purpose | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| will + base verb | Decision, promise, prediction | I'll send it after the call. |
| be going to + base verb | Prior intention or evidence-based prediction | We're going to revise the process. |
| Present continuous | Arranged future event | We're meeting the client on Tuesday. |
| Simple present | Fixed schedule | The session starts at nine. |

These uses overlap. Future continuous describes an expected ongoing arrangement: I'll be working from home tomorrow. Future perfect places completion before a future reference point: We'll have finished the review by Friday. Learn those two after the basics.

### 24.9 Modals: Ability, Requests, Obligation, and Judgment [Essential]

Modals take a base verb: She can help, not can helps or can to help.

| Form | Common functions | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| can / could | Ability, permission, requests, possibility | Could you check this? |
| would | Politeness, hypothetical situations, willingness | Would you be available tomorrow? |
| may / might | Possibility; may also expresses permission | This might take longer. |
| should | Advice or expectation | We should verify the totals. |
| must / have to | Strong obligation or necessity | We have to get approval first. |
| need to | Necessity | I need to check the details. |

Mustn't means prohibited; don't have to means unnecessary. You mustn't share the password is very different from You don't have to join the optional call. Must can also express strong inference: That must be the old version.

### 24.10 Direct and Subject Questions [Essential]

Ordinary questions use question word + auxiliary/be/modal + subject: When can you send it? Why did it fail?

Questions about the subject usually need no do: Who approved this? Compare the object question Who did you contact? Offer alternatives when helpful: Do you need a draft or the final version?

### 24.11 Embedded Questions and Polite Distance [Essential]

An embedded question uses statement order: Could you tell me when the meeting starts?, not when does the meeting start.

Use if/whether for an embedded yes/no question: Do you know whether we need approval? Here whether asks about uncertainty, not a condition. I was wondering if you could help can soften a current request; the past form does not necessarily restrict it to the past.

### 24.12 Negatives, Short Answers, Imperatives, and Tags [Essential / Developing]

Match the auxiliary in short answers: Can you join? Yes, I can. Have you sent it? No, I haven't.

Imperatives use the base verb: Please check the figures. Use Don't for a negative: Don't share that link publicly. Let's also takes the base form: Let's review it together.

Tags confirm expectations: It's ready, isn't it? You haven't sent it, have you? Avoid an ambiguous yes/no to a negative question by answering fully: That's right. I haven't sent it yet.

### 24.13 Articles, Countability, and Quantity [Essential]

A/an introduces one unspecified countable item. The identifies something the participants can recognize: I need a report. Please send the report we discussed. Choose a/an by sound: a user, an update.

Information, advice, feedback, equipment, and progress are normally uncountable. Use some feedback or a piece of advice; a suggestion is countable. Many/few go with countable plurals, much/little with uncountables, and a lot of with both.

A few issues means some issues; few issues emphasizes almost none. A little time means some time; little time emphasizes a shortage. The article changes the focus.

### 24.14 Pronouns and Reference [Essential]

Subject forms include I/we/they; object forms include me/us/them: Could you send it to me? Possessive determiners need a noun: my report. Possessive pronouns stand alone: This version is mine.

It refers to a known single item; this/that can refer to a current or previous idea. If there are two reports, say Update the budget report instead of Update it. Singular they can refer to someone whose gender is unknown: Someone left their notes here.

### 24.15 Adjectives, Adverbs, Comparison, and Degree [Essential]

An adjective describes something: The explanation is clear. An adverb describes the action: She explained it clearly.

Use faster than, more reliable than, as useful as, or the most practical. This version is more stable than the previous one. Do not say more easier.

Too means excessive: It's too risky. Enough follows an adjective: It's clear enough. Much better and slightly faster specify the degree of a comparison.

### 24.16 Time and Other Prepositions [Essential]

| Forms | Distinction | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| at / on / in | Clock time / day / longer period | at nine; on Monday; in October |
| by / until | Deadline / continuation | Send it by five. I'll be here until five. |
| in / for | Time from now / duration | I'll return in ten minutes. The call lasted for ten minutes. |
| during / while | Preposition + noun / conjunction + clause | during the meeting; while we were meeting |
| to / for | Recipient or direction / purpose or beneficiary | Send it to Maya. This is for the review. |

Learn fixed combinations: responsible for, depend on, agree with someone, focus on, access to, interested in. Do not translate prepositions word by word.

### 24.17 Infinitives and -ing Forms [Essential]

Want/need/plan/decide take to do: We decided to postpone the review. Avoid/finish/consider/mind take doing: We should avoid changing the scope again.

Prepositions normally take -ing: Thanks for checking. I'm looking forward to working with you has prepositional to. Let/make + person + base verb: Let me check. Ask/tell + person + to do: She asked me to revise it.

Stop doing ends an activity; stop to do interrupts one activity to perform another. Remember to send means remember the future action; remember sending means recall a past action.

### 24.18 Passive Voice and States [Essential]

Use be + past participle when the affected object matters more than the actor: The request was approved yesterday. The report is being reviewed. The fix has been deployed. It needs to be checked.

Do not hide ownership behind passives: Maya will review it is clearer for assignment than It will be reviewed. A form such as The door is closed may describe a state; not every be + participle describes a passive event.

### 24.19 Reason, Result, Purpose, Contrast, and Time Clauses [Essential]

- Because + clause: We postponed it because the data was incomplete.
- Because of + noun phrase: We postponed it because of the missing data.
- So + result: The data is incomplete, so we need to wait.
- To + base verb or so that + clause for purpose: I'll summarize it so that everyone can follow.
- Although/even though for contrast: Although it's faster, it's less reliable.
- When/before/after/as soon as for time: I'll let you know as soon as I hear back.

Do not mechanically pair because ... so ... or although ... but ... in one standard sentence structure. Future time clauses normally use a present form, not as soon as I will hear back.

### 24.20 Conditionals and Unless [Essential / Developing]

| Situation | Example | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regular consequence | If the password expires, the system asks for a reset. | A general rule. |
| Real future possibility | If we get approval today, we'll start tomorrow. | A feasible condition and consequence. |
| Hypothetical present or future | If we had more time, we could test another option. | An imagined alternative. |
| Counterfactual past | If we had checked earlier, we would have caught the error. | A past alternative that did not happen. |

Real future if clauses normally do not use will. Unless means if not: We won't release it unless the tests pass. Do not add another negative unintentionally. Mixed conditionals can come later: If we had planned better, we wouldn't be behind now connects past planning to the present result.

### 24.21 Content Clauses [Developing]

I think (that) we need more time embeds a thought; that is often omitted in speech. What we need is a clear decision uses a clause as the subject. The problem is that we don't have the latest data uses a clause as a complement.

Recognize the clause as a unit rather than stopping to name its category while speaking. Whether expresses uncertainty about yes/no; what asks for information: I don't know whether it's ready versus I don't know what they need.

### 24.22 Relative Clauses [Developing]

The colleague who handles billing is away identifies a person. The file that you sent is outdated identifies a thing. An object relative pronoun can be omitted: The file you sent.

A supplementary clause adds information and is set off with commas: The report, which Maya prepared, is ready. Do not normally use that in this kind of clause. Whose marks possession: Who is the person whose name is on the request?

### 24.23 Reported Speech and Backshift [Developing]

Maya said, "I'm reviewing it" can become Maya said she was reviewing it. Say does not normally take a person directly; tell normally does: She told me it was ready.

Past reporting often shifts tense, pronouns, and time references: will may become would, and today may become that day. Backshift is not always required when the fact remains current: She said the deadline is Friday. Report a request with She asked us to wait.

### 24.24 There Be and Anticipatory It [Essential]

There is a problem with the export and There are two options express existence. Do not say There have for these patterns.

It's important to confirm the date. It takes about an hour to run the checks. I find it difficult to estimate this task. In these patterns, it can serve a grammatical placeholder role rather than point to a specific object.

### 24.25 Past Perfect and Past Modal Meanings [Advanced]

Had + past participle puts an event before another past reference: By the time we joined, the meeting had started. Do not use had for every past event.

Should have checked expresses an unfulfilled obligation; could have prevented describes a past possibility; might have missed expresses uncertainty; must have misunderstood gives a strong inference. We might have missed a requirement does not make the same claim as We must have missed a requirement.

### 24.26 Phrasal Verbs, Two Objects, and Object Complements [Developing]

Separable phrasal verbs place pronoun objects in the middle: send it over, write it down, set it up, not set up it. Inseparable combinations stay together: look into it, not look it into.

Send me the file equals send the file to me, but explain the issue to me does not permit explain me the issue. Keep + object + state: Please keep me updated. Have/get something done can mean arrange for someone else to do it: We had the contract reviewed does not mean we personally reviewed it.

### 24.27 Ellipsis, Substitution, Parallelism, and Emphasis [Advanced]

Sounds good omits That. Shortened replies are normal, but explicit ownership still matters. I think so and I hope not substitute for earlier content.

Use matching forms in a list: We need to review the figures, update the chart, and send the report.

Emphasis: I do understand the concern. What we need is a decision. Literary inversion, complex participial constructions, and absolute constructions are lower-priority for ordinary workplace speech; learn to understand them without forcing them into conversation.

### 24.28 Numbers, Dates, Units, and Ranges [Essential]

Nine fifteen means 9:15. September 24 is often spoken as September twenty-fourth. Years may be grouped: twenty twenty-six. Name the month and time zone across regions.

Increase by 10% states the change; increase to 10% states the result. Say from ten to fifteen or between ten and fifteen, not between ten to fifteen. A two-hour meeting uses singular hour in the modifier; The meeting lasted two hours uses the plural noun. Read 0.5 as zero point five.

### 24.29 Tone, Contractions, and Stress [Essential]

Can you...? is an ordinary request. Could you...? is often softer. I was wondering if you could...? is more tentative but longer. Longer does not always mean more polite: a reason, reasonable deadline, and room to negotiate often matter more.

I'd like to expands to I would like to; I'd finished expands to I had finished. I'll is I will and I've is I have. The following verb form helps identify the contraction. Stress identifies corrections: I said **Friday**, not Thursday.

### 24.30 Priorities and Self-Check [Final Checklist]

| Level | Grammar focus | Practical outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start speaking accurately | Sentence patterns; be/do/have; present, past, continuous, basic future; questions and negatives; pronouns, articles, basic prepositions | Explain responsibilities, ask questions, and describe yesterday's and today's work. |
| Collaborate reliably | Present perfect; modals; embedded questions; real conditions; reasons and purpose; comparison; infinitives and -ing; basic passive | Request help, negotiate dates, explain blockers, and distinguish completed, ongoing, and expected work. |
| Explain complex situations | Perfect continuous; past perfect; relative and content clauses; reporting; hypothetical conditions; past modal meanings; ellipsis and emphasis | Review incidents, compare options, report opinions, and explain conditions and responsibility. |

Answer aloud before reading the key:

1. Report sending a document yesterday. Which tense fits?
2. Say the document has been sent and is now available to review.
3. Politely ask when the meeting starts using an embedded question.
4. Contrast no obligation to attend with a prohibition on attending.
5. State that approval today will let you start tomorrow.
6. Say you started waiting for feedback on Monday and are still waiting.
7. Contrast a Friday delivery deadline with continuing to wait until Friday.
8. Say you ought to have confirmed the requirements earlier but did not.

**Answer key:**

1. I sent the report yesterday. Use simple past for a finished past time.
2. I've sent the report. Please take a look. Present perfect emphasizes the current result.
3. Could you tell me when the meeting starts? Use statement order inside the question.
4. You don't have to attend means it is optional. You mustn't attend means it is prohibited.
5. If we get approval today, we'll start tomorrow. Use present tense in the ordinary real future condition.
6. I've been waiting for feedback since Monday. Perfect continuous emphasizes duration; since introduces the starting point.
7. Please send it by Friday. I'll wait until Friday. By marks the deadline; until marks continuation.
8. I should have confirmed the requirements first. Should have + past participle expresses an unfulfilled past obligation.

Do not wait to master every item before joining a meeting. Practice a real scenario, learn the grammar it requires, and repeat until you can communicate facts, requests, conditions, and commitments accurately.
